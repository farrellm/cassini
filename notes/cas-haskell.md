# Building a Computer Algebra System in Haskell: An Opinionated Reading & Building Guide

## TL;DR

- **Buy two books first and treat them as your spine:** Joel Cohen’s *Computer Algebra and Symbolic Computation* (2 vols., 2002/2003) for implementer-friendly expression/simplification/polynomial algorithms — note that the automatic-simplification **algorithm** is in Vol. 2 ch. 3, not Vol. 1, and Baader & Nipkow’s *Term Rewriting and All That* (1998) for the rewriting-engine theory (confluence, termination, AC-matching, Knuth–Bendix). Then use von zur Gathen & Gerhard’s *Modern Computer Algebra* as your deep algorithmic reference and Bronstein’s *Symbolic Integration I* when you reach integration.
- **Study Expreduce (Go) and Mathics (Python) as your architectural blueprints for the Wolfram evaluator**, and SymPy/GiNaC for the CAS-algorithms layer; for the Haskell layer specifically, read Hiromi Ishii’s paper + `computational-algebra`, Kmett’s `algebra`/`numeric-prelude` for the coefficient hierarchy, and adopt an **untyped single-`Expr` representation with recursion-schemes/uniplate** (the Wolfram/Lisp style) rather than GADTs for the core symbolic engine.
- **Scope realistically:** a minimal viable CAS is a term-rewriter with attributes (Flat/Orderless/HoldAll/Listable) + arbitrary-precision rationals + polynomial arithmetic + GCD + basic simplification + differentiation. Integration (Risch), factorization, and Gröbner bases are where hobby projects stall — build the rewriting kernel and bignum/polynomial substrate solidly first.

## Key Findings

The task splits cleanly into two independently learnable layers, and the literature reflects that split. For the **CAS-algorithms layer**, there is a small canon of textbooks that is genuinely sufficient; the hard part is not finding sources but sequencing them by difficulty and implementer-friendliness. For the **term-rewriting/evaluator layer**, the theory is well-covered by one book (Baader & Nipkow), but the practical “how does the Wolfram evaluator actually behave” knowledge lives in Wolfram’s own documentation, a handful of open-source reimplementations (Expreduce, Mathics, Symja), and the pattern-matching research literature (MatchPy and the AC-discrimination-net papers).

For Haskell specifically, the decisive design question is **typed vs. untyped expression representation**, and the evidence points to using an untyped `Expr` sum type for the Wolfram-style rewriting core (uniform matching, free `Eq`/`Ord`, matches “everything is `f[args]`”), while reserving strongly-typed GADT/type-level techniques for the polynomial/ring algebra sub-modules where they buy real safety (as Ishii’s `computational-algebra` demonstrates).

## Details

### Layer 1 — The CAS algorithms layer: the textbook canon

**Essential, start here — Joel S. Cohen, *Computer Algebra and Symbolic Computation* (two volumes).**

- Vol. 1, *Elementary Algorithms* (2002, ISBN 1-56881-158-6) and Vol. 2, *Mathematical Methods* (2003, ISBN 1-56881-159-4). Reprinted by Routledge/CRC; recent reprints carry ISBNs 9780367659479 (Methods) and the Elementary volume 9781568811581.
- This is the single most implementer-oriented CAS text. Cohen’s viewpoint is exactly the one you need: mathematical expressions are expression trees (the data objects), and a handful of primitive structural operations let you build automatic simplification, polynomial decomposition, and factorization. Algorithms are given in a “Mathematical Pseudo-Language” (MPL) with companion implementations in Maple, Mathematica, and MuPAD.
- Difficulty: moderate. Cohen states the prerequisites as the freshman–sophomore calculus sequence through multivariable calculus, elementary linear algebra and applied ODEs, plus a recommended discrete-mathematics course and some programming experience; “topics from elementary number theory and abstract algebra are introduced as needed,” so abstract algebra is *not* assumed.
- **Mind the split between the volumes — it is the opposite of what the titles suggest.** Vol. 1 gives you expression trees and the *shape* of an automatically simplified expression (ch. 2 Elementary Concepts, incl. §2.2 Expression Evaluation where automatic simplification is introduced; ch. 3 Recursive Structure of Mathematical Expressions, whose §3.2–§3.3 are Expression Structure and Trees and Structure-Based Operators; ch. 6 Structure of Polynomials and Rational Expressions). **The automatic-simplification algorithm itself is in Vol. 2, ch. 3** — §3.1 *The Goal of Automatic Simplification*, §3.2 *An Automatic Simplification Algorithm* — and Vol. 2 ch. 2 carries the integer/rational-arithmetic groundwork. If you are building the canonical-form engine, Vol. 2 is the volume you need in hand.

**Essential reference — von zur Gathen & Gerhard, *Modern Computer Algebra*, 3rd ed. (Cambridge, 2013, 808 pp., ISBN 9781107039032).**

- Cambridge University Press’s own blurb calls it “widely regarded as the ‘bible of computer algebra’.” Thorough, rigorous, with time-complexity analysis, complete proofs, and “implementation reports” on several topics. Beware a common mix-up about the editions: the **third** (2013) edition renovated Chapter 11, the Fast Euclidean Algorithm — a correctness bug had “squeezed through an unseen crack” in the proof — and corrected about 80 other errors, and that is all it claims. The new material in Chapters 3, 15 and 22 (which include GCD computation and symbolic integration) arrived with the **second**, 2003 edition — the copyright page reads “Cambridge University Press 1999, 2003”, and the authors’ own companion site lists “Second edition 2003: ISBN 978-0521826464” against “First edition 1999: ISBN 0521641764”. (February 2002 is the date on that edition’s preface, which is where the “2002 edition” miscitation comes from.)
- This is your reference for modular arithmetic, Chinese remainder, the fast Euclidean algorithm, Hensel lifting, factorization over finite fields, and evaluation/interpolation. Difficulty: high (graduate). Use it as a deep reference keyed off Cohen, not as a linear read. (Companion site: cosec.bit.uni-bonn.de, which carries the addenda and corrigenda and confirms the extent: 808 pages, 40 tables, 560 exercises.)

**Essential companion — Geddes, Czapor & Labahn, *Algorithms for Computer Algebra* (Kluwer, 1992, ISBN 0-7923-9259-0).**

- “The first comprehensive textbook” on the topic and still the best single volume that covers the *whole* pipeline an implementer needs: Normal Forms and Algebraic Representations; Arithmetic of Polynomials/Rational Functions/Power Series; Homomorphisms and Chinese Remainder; Newton’s Iteration and the Hensel Construction; Polynomial GCD Computation; Polynomial Factorization; Solving Systems of Equations (linear, fraction-free Gaussian elimination, nonlinear); Gröbner Bases; Integration of Rational Functions; and The Risch Integration Algorithm. Algorithms in Pascal-like pseudocode.
- **This is the book to own if you buy only one “algorithms” reference** — it is the most complete map of the GCD/factorization/integration territory the user asked about, at implementer-appropriate detail. Difficulty: moderate-to-high.

**Essential for integration — Manuel Bronstein, *Symbolic Integration I: Transcendental Functions*, 2nd ed. (Springer, 2005).**

- The standard reference for integration in finite terms of transcendental functions: Hermite reduction, the Rothstein–Trager and Lazard–Rioboo–Trager methods, and the Risch algorithm proper. Algorithms in ready-to-implement pseudocode; requires little beyond calculus and algebra. The 2nd edition adds a chapter on parallel integration (the Risch–Norman/parallel approach). Difficulty: high but exceptionally clearly written. Note the title says “I” — Bronstein died before completing the algebraic-functions Volume II, so the full algebraic case remains scattered across papers.

**Essential for summation — Petkovšek, Wilf & Zeilberger, *A=B* (A K Peters, 1996).**

- The book on algorithmic hypergeometric summation: Gosper’s algorithm, Zeilberger’s creative telescoping, the WZ method, and Petkovšek’s `Hyper`. Foreword by Knuth. **Freely and legally downloadable as a PDF from Herbert Wilf’s University of Pennsylvania page** (the authors made it free). Difficulty: moderate; wonderfully written. This covers the Gosper/Zeilberger part of the user’s summation requirements; Karr’s algorithm for symbolic summation in difference fields is not covered here and must be read from Karr’s papers / Schneider’s Sigma work.

**Essential for the rewriting layer — Baader & Nipkow, *Term Rewriting and All That* (Cambridge, 1998; pbk ISBN 0-521-77920-0 / 9780521779203, hbk 0-521-45520-0).**

- The self-contained introduction to term rewriting: abstract reduction systems, termination, confluence, critical pairs, completion (Knuth–Bendix), plus universal algebra, unification theory, and — usefully for a CAS — Gröbner bases and Buchberger’s algorithm. Main algorithms are given both informally and as Standard ML programs, with an ML primer in Appendix 2; the *efficient* unification and congruence-closure programs are developed in Pascal. Over 170 exercises.
- **Read the right chapters.** The chapter map is: 1 Motivating Examples, 2 Abstract Reduction Systems, 3 Universal Algebra, 4 Equational Problems (congruence closure, syntactic unification), **5 Termination**, **6 Confluence** (critical pairs, orthogonality), **7 Completion** (Knuth–Bendix, Huet’s procedure), 8 Gröbner Bases and Buchberger’s Algorithm, 9 Combination Problems, **10 Equational Unification** (§10.2 commutative, §10.3 associative-and-commutative), **11 Extensions** (§11.1 rewriting modulo equational theories, §11.3 conditional rewriting, §11.5 reduction strategies). The core for this project is **chs. 1–2 and 5–7**; chs. 10–11 become essential once you build the AC matcher. Chs. 1–4 alone will *not* give you termination, confluence, or completion.
- **This is the single best book for the evaluator/pattern-matching-theory layer.** Difficulty: moderate. Read it in parallel with Cohen.

**Optional / deeper theory:**

- **Richard Zippel, *Effective Polynomial Computation* (Kluwer, 1993; Kluwer International Series in Engineering and Computer Science 241; the Springer softcover reprint is ISBN 978-1-4613-6398-9).** Strong on the practical/theoretical tradeoffs in polynomial algorithms (Zippel is the originator of sparse modular “Zippel” interpolation for multivariate GCD). Good on where theoretically optimal algorithms are the wrong practical choice. Out of print but findable; ~363 pp. Optional but valuable when you build multivariate GCD.
- **Davenport, Siret & Tournier, *Computer Algebra: Systems and Algorithms for Algebraic Computation* (Academic Press, 2nd ed. 1993).** A gentler, systems-oriented survey with a preface by Daniel Lazard. Good conceptual overview; less of an implementation cookbook. Optional.
- **Terese, *Term Rewriting Systems* (Cambridge Tracts in TCS 55, 2003)** and **Klop’s survey “Term Rewriting Systems”** — the comprehensive advanced reference and the classic free survey, respectively. Optional; go here only if you need deep confluence/termination theory beyond Baader–Nipkow. Terese is in print. Klop’s full survey — abstract reduction systems, Knuth–Bendix completion, unification, orthogonal systems and reduction strategies, conditional TRSs — is the ~112-page chapter in *Handbook of Logic in Computer Science* Vol. 2 (Abramsky, Gabbay & Maibaum, eds., OUP 1992), circulated by CWI as report CS-R9053. **It is not freely obtainable** — the copy held here was supplied, and CS-R9053 could not be located free — but prefer the standalone PDF to the Handbook scan, which is images only and cannot be grepped at all. His shorter ICALP’90 survey “Term Rewriting Systems: From Church-Rosser to Knuth-Bendix and Beyond” (20 pp., also free from CWI) is the quick way in.

**Suggested reading order for Layer 1:** Cohen Vol. 1 chs. 2–3 (expression trees, the structure of simplified expressions) → Cohen Vol. 2 chs. 2–3 (rational arithmetic, then *the* automatic-simplification algorithm) → Baader & Nipkow chs. 1–2 and 5–7 (rewriting basics, in parallel) → Geddes/Czapor/Labahn (polynomial arithmetic → GCD → factorization) → von zur Gathen & Gerhard (fill in modular/Hensel/fast-arithmetic depth as needed) → Cohen Vol. 2 chs. 5–9 (polynomial decomposition/factorization from the implementer’s angle) → Baader & Nipkow chs. 10–11 (AC unification, rewriting modulo theories) → Bronstein (integration) → *A=B* (summation).

### Layer 2 — The Wolfram Language evaluator model

The authoritative behavioral spec is **Wolfram’s own documentation**, which is free and precise:

- The single most important page for Stage 1 is the *“Evaluation”* tech note (reference.wolfram.com/language/tutorial/Evaluation.html), whose section **“The Standard Evaluation Sequence”** gives the whole algorithm in order. **This is the spec — implement it literally.** For `h[e1, e2, …]`:

  1. If the expression is a raw object (`Integer`, `String`, …), leave it unchanged.
  2. Evaluate the head `h`.
  3. Evaluate each element `eᵢ` in turn.
  4. If `h` has **HoldFirst / HoldRest / HoldAll / HoldAllComplete**, skip evaluation of certain elements.
  5. Unless `h` has **SequenceHold** or HoldAllComplete, flatten out all `Sequence` objects among the `eᵢ`.
  6. Unless `h` has HoldAllComplete, strip the outermost of any `Unevaluated` wrappers among the `eᵢ`.
  7. If `h` has **Flat**, flatten out all nested expressions with head `h`.
  8. If `h` has **Listable**, thread through any `eᵢ` that are lists.
  9. If `h` has **Orderless**, sort the `eᵢ` into order.
  10. Unless `h` has HoldAllComplete, use any applicable rules **you have defined** for `h[f[e1,…],…]`.
  11. Use any **built-in** rules associated with `f` for `h[f[e1,…],…]`.
  12. Use any applicable rules **you have defined** for `h[e1,e2,…]` or `h[…][…]`.
  13. Use any **built-in** rules for `h[e1,e2,…]` or `h[…][…]`.

  And the **fixed-point loop**: “every time the expression changes, the Wolfram Language effectively starts the evaluation sequence over again.”
- Three things in that sequence are easy to get wrong and worth calling out. **Attribute order is Flat → Listable → Orderless** (steps 7–9), not any other permutation — flattening has to precede the canonical sort. **`Sequence` splicing precedes `Unevaluated` stripping** (steps 5–6). And the rule-application order is a **four-way** ladder (steps 10–13), not two independent axes: user upvalues → built-in upvalues → user downvalues → built-in downvalues. So upvalues beat downvalues, and within each level user definitions beat built-in ones — but note the consequence that **built-in upvalues beat *user* downvalues**. `SubValues` ride along with downvalues in steps 12–13, which is why `h[…][…]` appears there.
- *“Evaluation of Expressions”* (the longer tutorial) is the companion read: it gives a coarser six-step summary of the same procedure, the full attribute table, worked traces, and the prose statement of the precedence rule — “the Wolfram System always tries upvalue definitions before downvalue ones.” Use it for the explanations and the examples; use *“Evaluation”* for the algorithm.
- The attributes that matter for your engine: **HoldAll/HoldFirst/HoldRest** (control argument evaluation), **Flat** (associativity), **Orderless** (commutativity → canonical sorting), **Listable** (threading), **OneIdentity** (affects pattern matching — treat `f[a]` as `a`), plus `Protected`, `Constant`, `SequenceHold`. A useful thing to internalize from the same pages: `Hold`, `HoldComplete`, `HoldForm`, `ReleaseHold` and `Unevaluated` are *not* evaluator special-cases — they fall out of attributes plus ordinary definitions. (`Unevaluated[expr]` “effectively works by temporarily giving a function an attribute like HoldFirst.”)
- **OwnValues/DownValues/UpValues/SubValues** are the storage model for rules: OwnValues are the symbol’s own value (`x = 5`); DownValues attach rules to a symbol as head (`f[...] := ...`); UpValues attach rules to a symbol appearing as an *argument* (so `g` can define behavior for `f[g[...]]`); SubValues handle curried heads `f[x][y]` — the reference page defines them as “values for `f[…][…]…`”. Model these as four separate rule tables keyed by symbol. Don’t skip OwnValues: it is what makes plain assignment fall out of the same machinery as everything else.

**Stephen Wolfram’s own historical/design writings** (all free on stephenwolfram.com / writings.stephenwolfram.com) explain the philosophy behind the model and are worth reading for design taste, not algorithms:

- *“There Was a Time before Mathematica…”* (2013) — SMP’s design and the origin of the symbolic-expression + transformation-rule paradigm. Read carefully: the often-repeated line “quite a bit of that code is still running in Mathematica today, especially in the pattern matcher and evaluator” refers to the **Mathematica** code Wolfram wrote himself in 1986–88, *not* to SMP code. The essay is candid about SMP’s design being reworked rather than carried over — “SMP code is littered with `$` and `%`,” he had not yet had “the Mathematica idea of separating pattern constructs (such as `_`) from names.”
- *“Celebrating a Third of a Century of Mathematica…”* (2021) — the clearest statement of the core evaluation decision: “represent everything as a symbolic expression … represent all operations as transformations,” applied by “do the first transformation that applies … until nothing changes.”
- The original **SMP papers/manual** (Cole & Wolfram, “SMP: A Symbolic Manipulation Program,” ACM SYMSAC 1981; Greif, “The SMP Pattern-Matcher,” EUROCAL 1985, LNCS 204) are archived as PDFs on Wolfram’s content servers and are the earliest primary sources on how this pattern matcher was designed.

**Open-source reimplementations to study, by scale and lesson:**

- **Expreduce** (Cory Walker; Go; github.com/corywalker/expreduce) — **the single most relevant codebase for the user’s goal.** A from-scratch Wolfram-Language term-rewriter/CAS with a “fairly advanced” pattern matching engine implementing Blank/BlankSequence, Orderless/Flat matching, conditional rules, attributes, and the fixed-point evaluator. The author’s own README warns it is “experimental quality and is not currently intended for serious use,” which makes it *small and readable* — exactly what you want to reverse-engineer the evaluator loop. Lesson: how to structure the whole evaluator + matcher in a strongly-typed host language without Lisp’s dynamic tricks.
- **Mathics / Mathics3** (github.com/Mathics3/mathics-core; Python, GPL v3) — a more complete open Wolfram-Language kernel: a parser to build Expressions, an evaluator, and WL built-ins, delegating heavy math to SymPy and mpmath. Larger than Expreduce but very readable Python; the best reference for how built-in functions and the rule system are organized at scale. Lesson: a realistic module layout for a “batteries-included” WL kernel.
- **Symja** (Java; GPL v3 since 2.0.0, with LGPL parser/core modules; formerly MathEclipse) — a long-lived WL reimplementation with a mature parser and a large function library; worth grepping for specific algorithm implementations. Its README confirms it implements `Integrate` from the Rubi ruleset but names no version; per Symbolica’s writeup it is “the only other complete port,” based on **Rubi 4.16**.
- **MockMMA** (Richard Fateman, 1991) — of historical note as the earliest WL reimplementation. The widely repeated story that it drew a Wolfram cease-and-desist appears in Wikipedia’s Wolfram Language article **with no citation**; treat it as folklore until sourced. What *is* solidly available is a different document: Fateman’s **“A Review of Mathematica”** (*J. Symbolic Computation* 13(5), 1992) — not implementation notes but a sharp design critique of the language, the evaluator, and the pattern matcher from someone who had built a competing system. Read it for the criticism, not for MockMMA’s internals.
- **SymPy** (Python; BSD) — the most approachable *full* CAS to read. Pure-Python, with “no separate language” — as the reference paper puts it, “Unlike many CASs, SymPy does not invent its own programming language. Python itself is used both for the internal implementation and end user interaction.” The architecture overview is Meurer A. et al. (2017), “SymPy: symbolic computing in Python,” *PeerJ Computer Science* 3:e103, DOI 10.7717/peerj-cs.103 (24 authors). Lesson: how to organize a large CAS (assumptions system, `Basic`/`Expr` core, polys module) in a dynamically-typed host — and, via the DIVA-portal thesis and SymPy internals docs, how automatic-simplification decisions are made.
- **GiNaC** (C++; ginac.de; “GiNaC is Not a CAS”) — the Bauer/Frink/Kreckel *J. Symbolic Computation* (2002) paper is the canonical writeup. Lessons for you: (1) it deliberately abolishes the “low-level vs. high-level language” split (relevant to your Haskell-native ambition); (2) it uses **reference-counting with copy-on-write for structure sharing** (“an efficient memory management using reference counting and copy-on-write semantics”), and builds numbers on **CLN**; (3) clean polymorphic `ex`/`basic` class design with automatic normalization of rationals/complex. Study it for expression representation and memory management.
- **SymEngine** (C++ core with many language bindings incl. a thin Haskell `symengine` FFI package) — the fast C++ rewrite of SymPy’s core; lesson = the “fast typed core + scripting frontend” architecture, and a candidate to FFI into if you want a numerical/polynomial backend rather than writing your own.
- **Maxima** (Common Lisp; the open descendant of MIT Macsyma) and **Reduce** (Lisp) — the Lisp lineage. Reading Maxima teaches you why Lisp’s homoiconic s-expressions map so naturally onto CAS expression trees, and how a 50-year-old rule/simplifier (“general simplifier,” `tellsimp`/`defrule`) is structured. Large and old, but the Macsyma design is the ancestor of everything here (SMP itself was influenced by Macsyma, of which Wolfram was a user, and by Schoonschip, whose code he studied). Lesson: the untyped-expression paradigm in its native habitat.
- **Axiom / FriCAS** (Lisp core, SPAD algebra language; Jenks & Sutor, *AXIOM: The Scientific Computation System*, Springer 1992, ISBN 3-540-97855-0) — the opposite pole from Maxima: a **strongly-typed** CAS with a rigorous category/domain hierarchy (Ring, Field, etc. as first-class types). The open Axiom project is a **literate program** distributed as ~15 numbered “volumes” (Vol. 0 Jenks & Sutor; Vol. 10.x the algebra implementation/theory/categories/domains/packages), freely available as `.pamphlet` literate sources from github.com/daly/axiom/books — `axiom-developer.org` no longer resolves and no prebuilt volume PDFs survive. **This is essential reading for a Haskell implementer specifically**, because Axiom’s typed algebra hierarchy is the closest existing analog to what a type-driven Haskell CAS wants, and the literate volumes explain the *why*.
- **Singular, CoCoA, Macaulay2** (specialized: Gröbner bases / commutative algebra / algebraic geometry) and **PARI/GP** (number theory), **FLINT** (fast C library for number theory + polynomials, the modern high-performance substrate under Sage and others) — study these selectively when you need a specific algorithm done fast and correctly; FLINT in particular is the reference for state-of-the-art polynomial/bignum performance.
- **Symbolica** (Rust, with Python/C++ bindings; symbolica.io) — a modern, source-available high-performance CAS “built for large expressions.” Directly relevant for two reasons: (1) its pattern matcher, in Symbolica’s words, “supports commutative and associative matching, and has wildcards (variables ending in underscores) that can match to any subexpression” — a modern, fast take on Wolfram-style matching. (That sentence is from the *Symbolica 2.2* integration writeup; the separate pattern-matching post demonstrates the same thing as `is_symmetric=True` on a symbol, without using the word “commutative”.) (2) per Symbolica’s own writeup it provides “the only complete port of the latest Rubi 4.17 integration system outside the Wolfram Language, preserving its 7,000+ ordered rules and passing the complete 72,944-problem Rubi corpus” (in an MIT-licensed `symbolica-integrate` crate), processing that corpus “in 18 minutes of wall time, on a Ryzen 9 5900X parallelized over 8 cores” (57 ms median per problem). Lesson: rule-based integration (Rubi) as a pragmatic alternative/complement to Risch, and how to make AC-matching fast. (Note: Rubi’s own site, rulebasedintegration.org / Albert Rich’s Vision page, describes the ruleset as “over 6600 integration rules” and states that “Rubi 4.15 has 6,662 rules”; the “7,000+” figure is Symbolica’s count of its port of 4.17.)

### Pattern matching — the genuinely hard engineering

This is where a Wolfram-like engine lives or dies, and it is under-served by the CAS textbooks, so go to the rewriting/PM literature:

- **MatchPy** (Krebber, Barthels et al.; arXiv:1710.06915 and arXiv:1710.00077; open-source Python) — the best modern, readable treatment of “Mathematica-style” matching: syntactic matching plus **associative and/or commutative** functions, **sequence variables** (the BlankSequence/BlankNullSequence analogue), and constraints, with **many-to-one matching via discrimination nets**. The companion master’s thesis (arXiv:1705.00907, “Non-linear Associative-Commutative Many-to-One Pattern Matching with Sequence Variables”) is the most complete single document on the combined feature set. Key facts to internalize. Non-syntactic (AC) matching is **NP-hard** in general and worst-case exponential — the underlying result is Benanav, Kapur & Narendran, “Complexity of Matching Problems,” *J. Symbolic Computation* 3(1):203–216 (1987), which Krebber cites for AC matching being NP-complete; enumerating *all* matches is separately exponential in the subject size. Discrimination nets amortize a fixed pattern set across many subjects, though Krebber warns their state count “can grow exponentially with the number of patterns.” The conventional way to handle repeated variables is **two-stage**: nets “treat the patterns as if they were linear … in case of non-linear patterns, the equivalence of variable replacements has to be checked in an extra step.” Note that MatchPy itself **departs** from this — “we perform the full non-linear matching directly at a commutative symbol state instead of just using it as a filter.” **Both complexity results are Benanav’s**, not two separate papers: the same abstract that proves NP-completeness also shows that “if every variable appears at most once in a term being matched, then the associative-commutative matching problem is shown to have an upper-bound of *O*(|s|·|t|³)” — i.e. **linear AC matching is polynomial**. Their algorithm already used maximum bipartite graph matching. Eker’s “Associative-Commutative Matching via Bipartite Graph Matching” (*The Computer Journal* 38(5):381–399, 1995) is a *practical* algorithm for the general non-linear case — the lineage Maude is built on — and it explicitly credits Benanav for both bounds. Cite Benanav for the complexity, Eker for the algorithm.
- **AC-discrimination nets** — two distinct papers, and it is worth citing them apart. Bachmair, Chen & Ramakrishnan, “Associative-Commutative Discrimination Nets,” *TAPSOFT ’93* (CAAP/FASE, Orsay), pp. 61–74, introduces the data structure; Bachmair, Chen, Ramakrishnan, Anantharaman & Chabin, “Experiments with Associative-Commutative Discrimination Nets,” *IJCAI ’95*, pp. 348–354, is the measurement paper. Together they are the primary research on generalizing discrimination nets to AC symbols and on term indexing for Knuth–Bendix completion. Read them before you optimize your matcher, not before you write it.
- For the Haskell angle, note there is **no Haskell library with Mathematica-grade matching** off the shelf; you will implement it. The `dumb-cas` Hackage package is an interesting, small Haskell take on “untyped symbolic rewriting with user-defined rules, Lisp-flexibility + regex-conciseness,” worth reading as a starting point even though it is minimal.

### Rewriting-based language design — what to learn from the neighbors

- **Maude** (SRI; rewriting logic, membership equational logic) — the gold standard for *efficient built-in matching modulo* associativity/commutativity/identity, with a high-performance C++ engine and powerful reflection/metaprogramming. **This is the system to study for how to implement AC-matching and equational rewriting efficiently and correctly.**
- **OBJ / OBJ3** (Goguen) — the order-sorted equational-logic ancestor of Maude; foundational for the “programming = equational specification + rewriting” idea.
- **ELAN, Stratego, Tom** — the *strategy* languages: they separate rewrite *rules* from the *strategies* that control their application. The lesson for a Wolfram-like system is that this split maps onto the distinction between your rule tables and your evaluation control (`ReplaceRepeated`, `//.`, evaluation order, holding) — and that in practice the control layer is where the difficulty concentrates. (A frequently quoted line about “the strategies rather than the rewrite rules doing the heavy lifting” is *not* in the Rascal paper; don’t cite it there. What Rascal does show concretely is its `visit` construct carrying explicit `top-down`/`bottom-up` strategy annotations over an otherwise ordinary rule set.)
- **Rascal** (CWI) — meta-programming/language-workbench descendant of ASF+SDF; good for how to package rewriting into a usable language.
- **Pure** (Albert Gräf) — a modern, practical, *dynamically typed functional language based on term rewriting* with ML-style syntax; arguably the closest “small language built on term rewriting” to what the user is imagining, and readable.

### Expression representation, hash consing, memoization

- **Hash consing / structure sharing** is the central performance technique: intern structurally-equal subexpressions so equality is pointer comparison and identical subtrees are stored once. *“Efficient Symbolic Computation via Hash Consing”* (Zhu, Sabharwal, Tan, Ma, Edelman & Rackauckas; MIT and JuliaHub; arXiv:2509.20534) is an implementation report on adding hash-consing to JuliaSymbolics via a global **weak-reference** table, reporting up to 3.2× faster symbolic computation and 2× less memory. Its short related-work section is the useful survey for our purposes, and it is more negative about existing systems than is often reported: SymPy and SymEngine “stor[e] identical subexpressions in a set data structure, instead of … leveraging the DAG structure”; FriCAS and REDUCE get Lisp’s symbol interning but “we did not find a proper hash-consing implementation”; **GiNaC “does use a form of reference counting,” and GiNaC and Symbolica alike are called out because “it is trivial to construct programs using either package that demonstrate identical subexpressions with different memory locations”** — i.e. neither hash-conses. On the closed-source side it notes only that a Wolfram-internals page contains descriptions that “suggest hash-consing is used internally,” with “no description of the performance effects.” Reading across from that paper: their mechanism needs immutable terms plus a GC that can collect through weak references, which is precisely Haskell’s model — that inference is ours, not theirs, but it is a sound one. Design your `Expr` to be immutable and intern via a global (or `ST`/`IO`) hash table, or lean on Haskell’s sharing + a memo table for `simplify`.

### The Haskell-specific decisions

**Untyped vs. typed representation — the central call.** For the *Wolfram-style rewriting core*, use an **untyped single sum type**, e.g.
`data Expr = Int Integer | Rat Rational | Sym String | App Expr [Expr]` (everything is `f[args]`, mirroring Mathematica’s FullForm). This derives `Eq`/`Ord`/`Show` for free (needed for canonical ordering under Orderless and for memoization), supports uniform traversal/rewriting (via `uniplate` or `recursion-schemes`), and matches how every production Wolfram-like system (Maxima, Expreduce, Mathics) actually represents expressions. Christopher Olah’s **HaskSymb** experiment (github.com/colah/HaskSymb; blog post 1 June 2012) is a small, readable illustration of the untyped-plus-QuasiQuoter/ViewPatterns rewriting style — the blog post is only a demo of `expand`/`collectTerms`, so go to the repository README for the design retrospective. There he found no clean way to put variables into types: “The *big* issue I’m facing is appropriate types for symbolic expressions. In particular, how do I handle variables in types?”, settling on “My bad solution for now has been to just not have type-level variable representation, which kind of bothers me.” He wants dependent types and concludes Haskell cannot practically give him them here. Take the hint: **do not try to make the core symbolic engine fully type-safe.**

Reserve **GADTs / type-level techniques for the algebra sub-libraries** (polynomials, finite fields, ring towers) where types buy real safety. Hiromi Ishii’s **“A Purely Functional Computer Algebra System Embedded in Haskell”** (arXiv:1807.01456; CASC 2018, LNCS 11077, pp. 288–303, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-99639-4_20) and his **`computational-algebra`** library (github.com/konn/computational-algebra) are the reference: they encode polynomial arity, monomial ordering, and coefficient ring as type-level parameters (using promoted naturals, singletons, and a Presburger type-checker plugin — his `type-natural` and `ghc-typelits-presburger` packages) so that elements of ℚ[x,y,z] and ℚ[w,x,y] cannot be added by mistake, and implement Gröbner bases with **F4, F5, and Hilbert-driven** algorithms.  Lesson: strong typing is worth it for the ring algebra; it needs heavy machinery and property-based testing (QuickCheck) because the type system checks arity/identity but **not** the ring *axioms*. Ishii positions this explicitly against **DoCon** (Mechveliani), the pioneering Haskell CAS, “with more emphasis on safety and correctness.”

**The algebraic-typeclass-hierarchy problem.** Haskell’s standard `Num` is inadequate for a CAS, for reasons the `numeric-prelude` README states plainly (these four objections are in the Hackage package README, not on the HaskellWiki page): it “defines no semantics for the fundamental operations” (e.g. nothing says addition is associative); it forces `Eq` and `Show` as superclasses (impossible to give non-bottom instances for, e.g., a function-valued ring like `data IntegerFunction a = IF (a -> Integer)`); and it lumps semantic operations together with representation-specific ones (`toInteger`, `decodeFloat`) and is “not fine-grained enough.” Two mature replacements:

- **`numeric-prelude`** (Thielemann et al.; Hackage) — an experimental alternative hierarchy splitting `Num` into `Additive`, `Ring`, `Field`, `Algebraic`, `Transcendental`, etc.  (mapping `Num → Additive, Ring, Absolute`; `Fractional → Field`; `Floating → Algebraic, Transcendental`; and so on). The Hackage README is the canonical statement of *why* `Num` is wrong, and carries the migration table verbatim; the HaskellWiki page adds the structure list and the honest *Future plans* section, which admits “the code still misses proper linear algebra code” and unsolved static-checking problems for “residue classes, matrix computations, infinite precision numbers, fixed point numbers.” Axioms are stated as QuickCheck properties.
- **`algebra`** (Edward Kmett; Hackage/github.com/ekmett/algebra) — a finer-grained hierarchy (magmas → semigroups → groups → rings → modules → algebras, with additive/multiplicative distinctions) that Ishii’s CAS builds on. `numhask` (Tony Day) is a cleaner, more modern alternative. (Both rely on `RebindableSyntax` — numeric-prelude’s own README prescribes it — so that is not what distinguishes them; numhask’s case is a smaller, more recently maintained hierarchy.)
  Use one of these as your coefficient tower rather than rolling your own or fighting `Num`.

**Extensibility — the expression problem.** If you want to add new expression-node kinds and new operations without editing existing code, the reference is Wouter Swierstra’s **“Data types à la carte”** (*JFP* 18(4):423–436, 2008, DOI 10.1017/S0956796808006758), which composes ASTs as coproducts of functors (`data (f :+: g) e = Inl (f e) | Inr (g e)`) tied together with `Fix`, plus a subtyping class `(:<:)` for injection. Phil Wadler’s verdict — on his blog, 28 February 2008, not in the paper itself, which cites him only as the problem’s namer — was that it “presents the best solution to the Expression Problem that I’ve seen in Haskell (well, Haskell with `-fglasgow-exts`).”  Matthew Pickering’s closed-type-family reimplementation and Patrick Bahr’s “Compositional Data Types” are the productionized descendants. In practice, for a Wolfram-like system where *everything is `f[args]` anyway*, you may not need à la carte at all — the untyped `Expr` sidesteps the expression problem by making the node set uniform. Use `recursion-schemes` (`cata`/`ana` over a base functor; Kmett’s library, or `data-fix`) or the `Free` monad (Chris Penner’s “ASTs with Fix and Free”; Bartosz Milewski’s higher-algebra posts) for clean folds/evaluators and for substitution.

**Other Haskell libraries worth reading as reference** (from the ecosystem survey): `poly` (Bodigrim) — fast `Vector`-backed uni/multivariate polynomials with Karatsuba multiplication,  slotting into the `semirings` `GcdDomain`/`Euclidean` classes (its maintainer benchmarks it “at least 20x–40x faster than the `polynomial` library” — a self-reported figure); `constructive-algebra` (Mörtberg et al.) — small, very readable constructive ring/ideal/matrix code (coherent rings without Noetherian assumptions); `sbv` (Erkök) — how to make symbolic values overload `Num`/`Ord` (`.==`, `.>`) and offload to an SMT solver like Z3 (useful for zero-testing side conditions, not for rewriting); `symengine` — thin FFI bindings if you want a fast external core; and the historical **DoCon** (Mechveliani), which used runtime “sample arguments” for ring identity — the design Ishii explicitly improves on. 

### Zero-testing and the undecidability wall

A conceptual point every implementer must internalize: **there is no general algorithm for deciding whether a symbolic expression is zero.** Richardson’s theorem — Daniel Richardson, “Some **Undecidable** Problems Involving Elementary Functions of a Real Variable,” *Journal of Symbolic Logic* 33(4):514–520 (1968) — shows that for the class of expressions generated by the rational numbers, π, ln 2, the variable x, addition/subtraction/multiplication/composition, and the `sin`, `exp`, and `abs` functions, the predicate “E = 0?” is unsolvable. (The title is routinely miscited as “Unsolvable”; the paper is “Undecidable”.) The `abs` is not decoration: Richardson’s identity result needs condition (1), that the function class contain log 2, π, eˣ and sin x, **and** condition (2), that it contain some μ with μ(x) = |x| for x ≠ 0 — he notes √x² will do. Drop condition (2) and it is the *integration* problem, under a further condition (3), that goes undecidable instead. This is why every real CAS’s simplifier is a bundle of heuristics with a “good enough” contract rather than a decision procedure, why `Simplify` can’t be complete, and why canonical forms are only defined for restricted classes (polynomials, rational functions). Build your simplifier as a *confluent-as-possible, terminating* rewrite system with an explicit normal form for the sub-domains where one exists (rationals, polynomials), and treat general simplification as best-effort. (Baader & Nipkow give you the confluence/termination tools; the undecidability result tells you where the ceiling is.)

### University course materials and worked build-logs

Course materials that pair theory with build-it projects are scattered but real: von zur Gathen & Gerhard maintain a companion site for *Modern Computer Algebra* (cosec.bit.uni-bonn.de). The DIVA-portal MSc thesis “Constructing a Computer Algebra System Capable of…” (extending SymPy) and Victor Brun’s “Building a Computer Algebra System in Go, Part 1” (2022; only Part 1 appears to exist — no later installments are discoverable) are good worked examples of scoping a from-scratch build. Fredrik Johansson’s (FLINT/Arb author) blog post “Things I would like to see in a computer algebra system” is a valuable practitioner’s list of what matters — including his point that “the core datatype in computer algebra is the arbitrary-size integer” while “hardware, compilers, programming languages are not at all designed for” it — a direct argument for getting your bignum/GMP substrate right first.

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Foundations (before writing the engine).** Read Cohen Vol. 1 chs. 2–3 (expression trees, the structure of a simplified expression) **and Vol. 2 chs. 2–3 (rational arithmetic, and the automatic-simplification algorithm — it is in Vol. 2, not Vol. 1)**, plus Baader & Nipkow chs. 1–2 and 5–7 (abstract reduction systems, termination, confluence, completion). Decide representation now: **untyped `Expr` sum type, immutable, with hash-consing**. Get a solid arbitrary-precision rational layer working first (Haskell’s `Integer` already uses GMP; add `Ratio`). Benchmark threshold to proceed: you can construct, hash-cons, and structurally compare large expressions cheaply.

**Stage 1 — Minimal viable CAS core.** Build the evaluator loop and matcher: (1) the fixed-point evaluate-until-unchanged loop, with rule application as the **four-way ladder** the docs specify — user upvalues → built-in upvalues → user downvalues → built-in downvalues. These are not two independent axes: built-in upvalues beat *user* downvalues, so a two-axis implementation gets this case wrong; (2) attributes Flat, Orderless (with canonical sort), Listable, HoldAll/HoldFirst/HoldRest, OneIdentity; (3) pattern matching with Blank/BlankSequence/BlankNullSequence, conditional (`/;`) rules, and non-linear patterns; (4) OwnValues/DownValues/UpValues rule tables (SubValues when you want curried heads); (5) automatic simplification of `Plus`/`Times`/`Power` to a canonical form over rationals; (6) symbolic differentiation (the classic easy win). This is a genuine Wolfram-Language-subset kernel. **Study Expreduce’s source in parallel — it is your closest blueprint.** Benchmark threshold: your kernel reproduces the standard `D[...]` product/chain rules and sorts/flattens `a+b+a` → `2 a + b`.

**Stage 2 — Polynomial substrate.** Implement a real polynomial representation (start sparse-distributed for multivariate; add recursive/dense as needed), then GCD: Euclidean → subresultant PRS → modular/Brown’s (dense) and Zippel’s sparse interpolation for multivariate. This is where you *may* introduce Ishii-style typed polynomials as a separate, strongly-typed module. Follow Geddes/Czapor/Labahn and von zur Gathen & Gerhard. Benchmark: correct multivariate GCD and content/primitive-part on non-trivial inputs.

**Stage 3 — Factorization and the hard algorithms.** Factorization over finite fields (Berlekamp, Cantor–Zassenhaus) → Hensel lifting → Zassenhaus over ℤ → (optionally) van Hoeij/LLL. Then Gröbner bases (Buchberger first, then F4). Then integration: Hermite reduction + Rothstein–Trager for the rational part, then the transcendental Risch case from Bronstein; consider porting Rubi rules (as Symbolica/Symja did) as a pragmatic heuristic integrator alongside or before full Risch. This is the stage where **hobby projects stall** — sequence it last and accept partial coverage.

**Where projects typically die, and how to avoid it:** (a) trying to make the core type-safe and drowning in type-level machinery — *don’t; keep the core untyped*; (b) underestimating automatic simplification (the “boring” canonical-form logic is the actual hard part of feeling like a CAS) — *budget for it, and lean on Cohen*; (c) building integration/factorization before the polynomial/GCD/bignum substrate is solid — *invert the order*; (d) ignoring pattern-matcher performance until the rule set is large — *write a correct one-to-one AC matcher first, but put it behind an interface that can be swapped for a discrimination net later*. Don’t build the net up front: Krebber’s own measurements show many-to-one paying off only once the pattern set is large, and warn that a net’s state count “can grow exponentially with the number of patterns”; (e) no memoization/structure-sharing — *hash-cons from day one*.

**Two-book minimum if budget-constrained:** Cohen **Vol. 2** + Baader & Nipkow — Vol. 2 is the one with the automatic-simplification algorithm and the rational arithmetic, which is what a minimum buy is for. Add Vol. 1 as soon as you can afford it for the expression-tree material. **Free-PDF starting kit:** *A=B* (released by the authors), the Wolfram evaluation docs, the MatchPy and Ishii arXiv papers, Klop’s shorter ICALP’90 survey from CWI (the *full* Term Rewriting Systems survey is **not** free), the Axiom `.pamphlet` volumes from github.com/daly/axiom/books, and the SymPy (PeerJ, open access) architecture paper.

## Caveats

- **“Read the source” varies wildly in payoff.** Expreduce and Mathics are small/readable and directly on-target; Maxima, Axiom, and Sage are huge and old, and reading them cover-to-cover is not a good use of time — grep them for specific algorithms.
- **Bronstein Volume II never appeared** (the author died); the algebraic-function integration case remains scattered across research papers, so “full Risch” for algebraic functions is genuinely frontier work, not a textbook exercise.
- **Karr’s summation algorithm** (difference fields) is not in *A=B*; for that specific requirement you need Karr, “Summation in Finite Terms,” *JACM* 28(2):305–350 (1981) — the discrete analogue of Risch — or, more approachably, Carsten Schneider’s Sigma work (“Symbolic Summation Assists Combinatorics,” *Sém. Lothar. Combin.* 56, B56b, free from RISC).
- **Symbolica is source-available but commercially licensed.** The trigger is employment, not commerce: “Symbolica is free for hobbyist use. If you use Symbolica as part of your employment, whether in academia or in a commercial or non-commercial organization, a license is required,” with the free tier capped at “one core and instance per device” and redistribution requiring written permission. Fine to read and learn from; check the license before depending on it. Its Rubi-corpus and performance figures (72,944 problems in 18 minutes; “7,000+ rules”) are the vendor’s own reported benchmarks, and Rubi’s own site describes the ruleset as “over 6600” rules (“Rubi 4.15 has 6,662 rules”).
- **The claim that Wolfram uses hash-consing internally is inferred, not confirmed** — the Wolfram kernel is closed-source; treat internals writeups (including reverse-engineering blog posts and the community “Wolfram Language Specification” project at wltools.github.io) as informed inference, not authoritative.
- **Typed-CAS design is an open research problem.** Ishii’s and Axiom’s typed hierarchies are impressive but neither is a settled “right answer”; expect pragmatic compromises, and note that even Ishii verifies ring axioms only by property testing, not by proof.
- **This document has been validated against the local corpus in `references/`.** Claims sourced there are marked by the specificity of their citations; a handful rest on live sources checked at the same time (Expreduce’s and `poly`’s READMEs, Wadler’s blog, the Symja repository, HaskSymb’s README). Two things remain genuinely unverified and are flagged inline: Zippel’s original Kluwer hardback ISBN, and whether `numeric-prelude` relies on multi-parameter type classes.
- **Corpus caveats worth knowing before you go looking.** The Terese scan in `references/` is image-only and has no text layer, so it cannot be grepped. The Wolfram `ref/` HTML captures for DownValues/UpValues/SubValues/Attributes preserved little beyond the one-line definitions; the substantive captures are the *Evaluation of Expressions* tutorial (the full attribute table and worked traces) and *Transformation Rules and Definitions* (`wolfram_ref_associating_definitions.html`), which is where the rule-storage model is actually explained. Both editions of the Axiom book are held and they are different files: `jenks_sutor1992_axiom.pdf` is the genuine Springer 1992 edition (765 pp., full text layer) and is the one to cite; `fricas2026_system_for_computer_mathematics.pdf` is the FriCAS project’s modern regeneration — close in content, but cite it as FriCAS.
