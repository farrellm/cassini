# Building a Computer Algebra System in Haskell: An Opinionated Reading & Building Guide

## TL;DR

- **Buy two books first and treat them as your spine:** Joel Cohen’s *Computer Algebra and Symbolic Computation* (2 vols., 2002/2003) for implementer-friendly expression/simplification/polynomial algorithms, and Baader & Nipkow’s *Term Rewriting and All That* (1998) for the rewriting-engine theory (confluence, termination, AC-matching, Knuth–Bendix). Then use von zur Gathen & Gerhard’s *Modern Computer Algebra* as your deep algorithmic reference and Bronstein’s *Symbolic Integration I* when you reach integration.
- **Study Expreduce (Go) and Mathics (Python) as your architectural blueprints for the Wolfram evaluator**, and SymPy/GiNaC for the CAS-algorithms layer; for the Haskell layer specifically, read Hiromi Ishii’s paper + `computational-algebra`, Kmett’s `algebra`/`numeric-prelude` for the coefficient hierarchy, and adopt an **untyped single-`Expr` representation with recursion-schemes/uniplate** (the Wolfram/Lisp style) rather than GADTs for the core symbolic engine.
- **Scope realistically:** a minimal viable CAS is a term-rewriter with attributes (Flat/Orderless/HoldAll/Listable) + arbitrary-precision rationals + polynomial arithmetic + GCD + basic simplification + differentiation. Integration (Risch), factorization, and Gröbner bases are where hobby projects stall — build the rewriting kernel and bignum/polynomial substrate solidly first.

## Key Findings

The task splits cleanly into two independently learnable layers, and the literature reflects that split. For the **CAS-algorithms layer**, there is a small canon of textbooks that is genuinely sufficient; the hard part is not finding sources but sequencing them by difficulty and implementer-friendliness. For the **term-rewriting/evaluator layer**, the theory is well-covered by one book (Baader & Nipkow), but the practical “how does the Wolfram evaluator actually behave” knowledge lives in Wolfram’s own documentation, a handful of open-source reimplementations (Expreduce, Mathics, Symja), and the pattern-matching research literature (MatchPy and the AC-discrimination-net papers).

For Haskell specifically, the decisive design question is **typed vs. untyped expression representation**, and the evidence points to using an untyped `Expr` sum type for the Wolfram-style rewriting core (uniform matching, free `Eq`/`Ord`, matches “everything is `f[args]`”), while reserving strongly-typed GADT/type-level techniques for the polynomial/ring algebra sub-modules where they buy real safety (as Ishii’s `computational-algebra` demonstrates).

## Details

### Layer 1 — The CAS algorithms layer: the textbook canon

**Essential, start here — Joel S. Cohen, *Computer Algebra and Symbolic Computation* (two volumes).**

- Vol. 1, *Elementary Algorithms* (2002, ISBN 1-56881-158-6) and Vol. 2, *Mathematical Methods* (2003). Reprinted by Routledge/CRC; recent reprints carry ISBNs 9780367659479 (Methods) and the Elementary volume 9781568811581.
- This is the single most implementer-oriented CAS text. Cohen’s viewpoint is exactly the one you need: mathematical expressions are expression trees (the data objects), and a handful of primitive structural operations let you build automatic simplification, polynomial decomposition, and factorization. Algorithms are given in a “Mathematical Pseudo-Language” (MPL) with companion implementations in Maple, Mathematica, and MuPAD.
- Difficulty: moderate; minimal prerequisites (one undergrad abstract-algebra course). **This is the book that most directly teaches you how to build the guts of automatic simplification and canonical forms** — the notoriously fiddly part most tutorials skip. The first volume’s TOC (Recursive Structure of Mathematical Expressions; Structure-Based Operators; Elementary Mathematical Algorithms) maps almost 1:1 onto the modules you will write.

**Essential reference — von zur Gathen & Gerhard, *Modern Computer Algebra*, 3rd ed. (Cambridge, 2013, 808 pp., ISBN 9781107039032).**

- Cambridge University Press’s own blurb calls it “widely regarded as the ‘bible of computer algebra’.” Thorough, rigorous, with time-complexity analysis, complete proofs, and “implementation reports” on several topics. Third edition added new material on GCDs and symbolic integration and renovated the Fast Euclidean Algorithm chapter.
- This is your reference for modular arithmetic, Chinese remainder, the fast Euclidean algorithm, Hensel lifting, factorization over finite fields, and evaluation/interpolation. Difficulty: high (graduate). Use it as a deep reference keyed off Cohen, not as a linear read. (Companion site: cosec.bit.uni-bonn.de.)

**Essential companion — Geddes, Czapor & Labahn, *Algorithms for Computer Algebra* (Kluwer, 1992, ISBN 0-7923-9259-0).**

- “The first comprehensive textbook” on the topic and still the best single volume that covers the *whole* pipeline an implementer needs: Normal Forms and Algebraic Representations; Arithmetic of Polynomials/Rational Functions/Power Series; Homomorphisms and Chinese Remainder; Newton’s Iteration and the Hensel Construction; Polynomial GCD Computation; Polynomial Factorization; Gröbner Bases; Integration of Rational Functions; and The Risch Integration Algorithm. Algorithms in Pascal-like pseudocode.
- **This is the book to own if you buy only one “algorithms” reference** — it is the most complete map of the GCD/factorization/integration territory the user asked about, at implementer-appropriate detail. Difficulty: moderate-to-high.

**Essential for integration — Manuel Bronstein, *Symbolic Integration I: Transcendental Functions*, 2nd ed. (Springer, 2005).**

- The standard reference for integration in finite terms of transcendental functions: Hermite reduction, the Rothstein–Trager and Lazard–Rioboo–Trager methods, and the Risch algorithm proper. Algorithms in ready-to-implement pseudocode; requires little beyond calculus and algebra. The 2nd edition adds a chapter on parallel integration (the Risch–Norman/parallel approach). Difficulty: high but exceptionally clearly written. Note the title says “I” — Bronstein died before completing the algebraic-functions Volume II, so the full algebraic case remains scattered across papers.

**Essential for summation — Petkovšek, Wilf & Zeilberger, *A=B* (A K Peters, 1996).**

- The book on algorithmic hypergeometric summation: Gosper’s algorithm, Zeilberger’s creative telescoping, the WZ method, and Petkovšek’s `Hyper`. Foreword by Knuth. **Freely and legally downloadable as a PDF from Herbert Wilf’s University of Pennsylvania page** (the authors made it free). Difficulty: moderate; wonderfully written. This covers the Gosper/Zeilberger part of the user’s summation requirements; Karr’s algorithm for symbolic summation in difference fields is not covered here and must be read from Karr’s papers / Schneider’s Sigma work.

**Essential for the rewriting layer — Baader & Nipkow, *Term Rewriting and All That* (Cambridge, 1998, ISBN 0-521-77920-0 pbk).**

- The self-contained introduction to term rewriting: abstract reduction systems, termination, confluence, critical pairs, completion (Knuth–Bendix), plus universal algebra, unification theory, and — usefully for a CAS — Gröbner bases and Buchberger’s algorithm as an instance of completion. Main algorithms given both informally and as Standard ML programs; unification and congruence closure are developed in depth with efficient code. 170+ exercises.
- **This is the single best book for the evaluator/pattern-matching-theory layer.** Difficulty: moderate. Read it in parallel with Cohen.

**Optional / deeper theory:**

- **Richard Zippel, *Effective Polynomial Computation* (Kluwer, 1993, ISBN 0-7923-9375-9).** Strong on the practical/theoretical tradeoffs in polynomial algorithms (Zippel is the originator of sparse modular “Zippel” interpolation for multivariate GCD). Good on where theoretically optimal algorithms are the wrong practical choice. Out of print but findable; ~363 pp. Optional but valuable when you build multivariate GCD.
- **Davenport, Siret & Tournier, *Computer Algebra: Systems and Algorithms for Algebraic Computation* (Academic Press, 2nd ed. 1993).** A gentler, systems-oriented survey with a preface by Daniel Lazard. Good conceptual overview; less of an implementation cookbook. Optional.
- **Terese, *Term Rewriting Systems* (Cambridge Tracts in TCS 55, 2003)** and **Klop’s lecture notes “Term Rewriting Systems”** — the comprehensive advanced reference and the classic free lecture notes, respectively. Optional; go here only if you need deep confluence/termination theory beyond Baader–Nipkow. (Klop’s notes circulate free online; Terese is in print.)

**Suggested reading order for Layer 1:** Cohen Vol. 1 (build simplification + expression trees) → Baader & Nipkow chs. 1–4 (rewriting basics, in parallel) → Geddes/Czapor/Labahn (polynomial arithmetic → GCD → factorization) → von zur Gathen & Gerhard (fill in modular/Hensel/fast-arithmetic depth as needed) → Cohen Vol. 2 (polynomial decomposition/factorization from the implementer’s angle) → Bronstein (integration) → *A=B* (summation).

### Layer 2 — The Wolfram Language evaluator model

The authoritative behavioral spec is **Wolfram’s own documentation**, which is free and precise:

- *“Evaluation of Expressions”* and *“Evaluation”* (reference.wolfram.com) give the actual standard evaluation procedure step by step. The key sequence: evaluate the head; if the head has a Hold attribute (HoldFirst/HoldRest/HoldAll/HoldAllComplete) the relevant arguments are held; strip `Unevaluated` wrappers (unless HoldAllComplete); flatten `Sequence` objects (unless SequenceHold); then, immediately after evaluating elements, apply **Flat** (flatten nested same-head expressions), **Listable** (thread over lists), and **Orderless** (sort arguments into canonical order); then apply upvalues associated with the arguments, then downvalues/built-in definitions associated with the head; the result is re-evaluated until it no longer changes (**fixed-point loop**).
- The attributes that matter for your engine: **HoldAll/HoldFirst/HoldRest** (control argument evaluation), **Flat** (associativity), **Orderless** (commutativity → canonical sorting), **Listable** (threading), **OneIdentity** (affects pattern matching — treat `f[a]` as `a`), plus `Protected`, `Constant`, `SequenceHold`. The riptutorial “Evaluation Order” page gives a clean, concise pseudo-algorithm of the whole loop and correctly notes that functions like `Hold`, `ReleaseHold`, `ReplacePart` are *not* evaluator special-cases but fall out of attributes + ordinary definitions.
- **UpValues/DownValues/SubValues** are the storage model for rules: DownValues attach rules to a symbol as head (`f[...] := ...`); UpValues attach rules to a symbol appearing as an *argument* (so `g` can define behavior for `f[g[...]]`); SubValues handle curried heads `f[x][y]`. Model these as separate rule tables keyed by symbol.

**Stephen Wolfram’s own historical/design writings** (all free on stephenwolfram.com / writings.stephenwolfram.com) explain the philosophy behind the model and are worth reading for design taste, not algorithms:

- *“There Was a Time before Mathematica…”* (2013) — SMP’s design and the origin of the symbolic-expression + transformation-rule paradigm; notably admits early SMP pattern-matcher/evaluator code still runs in Mathematica.
- *“Celebrating a Third of a Century of Mathematica…”* (2021) — the clearest statement of the core evaluation decision: “represent everything as a symbolic expression … represent all operations as transformations,” applied by “do the first transformation that applies … until nothing changes.”
- The original **SMP papers/manual** (Cole & Wolfram, “SMP: A Symbolic Manipulation Program,” ACM SYMSAC 1981; Greif, “The SMP Pattern-Matcher,” EUROCAL 1985, LNCS 204) are archived as PDFs on Wolfram’s content servers and are the earliest primary sources on how this pattern matcher was designed.

**Open-source reimplementations to study, by scale and lesson:**

- **Expreduce** (Cory Walker; Go; github.com/corywalker/expreduce) — **the single most relevant codebase for the user’s goal.** A from-scratch Wolfram-Language term-rewriter/CAS with a “fairly advanced” pattern matching engine implementing Blank/BlankSequence, Orderless/Flat matching, conditional rules, attributes, and the fixed-point evaluator. The author’s own README warns it is “experimental quality and is not currently intended for serious use,” which makes it *small and readable* — exactly what you want to reverse-engineer the evaluator loop. Lesson: how to structure the whole evaluator + matcher in a strongly-typed host language without Lisp’s dynamic tricks.
- **Mathics / Mathics3** (github.com/Mathics3/mathics-core; Python, GPL v3) — a more complete open Wolfram-Language kernel: a parser to build Expressions, an evaluator, and WL built-ins, delegating heavy math to SymPy and mpmath. Larger than Expreduce but very readable Python; the best reference for how built-in functions and the rule system are organized at scale. Lesson: a realistic module layout for a “batteries-included” WL kernel.
- **Symja** (Java) — the oldest actively maintained WL reimplementation (formerly MathEclipse); mature parser and a large function library; worth grepping for specific algorithm implementations. It is also one of only two complete ports of the Rubi integration ruleset (its converter targets Rubi 4.16).
- **MockMMA** (Richard Fateman, 1991) — of historical note as the first WL reimplementation (and famously received a Wolfram cease-and-desist); Fateman’s accompanying notes on implementation are instructive.
- **SymPy** (Python; BSD) — the most approachable *full* CAS to read. Pure-Python, with “no separate language” — as the reference paper puts it, “Unlike many CASs, SymPy does not invent its own programming language. Python itself is used both for the internal implementation and end user interaction.” The architecture overview is Meurer A. et al. (2017), “SymPy: symbolic computing in Python,” *PeerJ Computer Science* 3:e103, DOI 10.7717/peerj-cs.103 (28 authors). Lesson: how to organize a large CAS (assumptions system, `Basic`/`Expr` core, polys module) in a dynamically-typed host — and, via the DIVA-portal thesis and SymPy internals docs, how automatic-simplification decisions are made.
- **GiNaC** (C++; ginac.de; “GiNaC is Not a CAS”) — the Bauer/Frink/Kreckel *J. Symbolic Computation* (2002) paper is the canonical writeup. Lessons for you: (1) it deliberately abolishes the “low-level vs. high-level language” split (relevant to your Haskell-native ambition); (2) it uses **reference-counting with copy-on-write for structure sharing**, and builds numbers on **CLN** (on top of GMP); (3) clean polymorphic `ex`/`basic` class design with automatic normalization of rationals/complex. Study it for expression representation and memory management.
- **SymEngine** (C++ core with many language bindings incl. a thin Haskell `symengine` FFI package) — the fast C++ rewrite of SymPy’s core; lesson = the “fast typed core + scripting frontend” architecture, and a candidate to FFI into if you want a numerical/polynomial backend rather than writing your own.
- **Maxima** (Common Lisp; the open descendant of MIT Macsyma) and **Reduce** (Lisp) — the Lisp lineage. Reading Maxima teaches you why Lisp’s homoiconic s-expressions map so naturally onto CAS expression trees, and how a 50-year-old rule/simplifier (“general simplifier,” `tellsimp`/`defrule`) is structured. Large and old, but the Macsyma design is the ancestor of everything here (SMP itself was influenced by Macsyma, of which Wolfram was a user, and by Schoonschip, whose code he studied). Lesson: the untyped-expression paradigm in its native habitat.
- **Axiom / FriCAS** (Lisp core, SPAD algebra language; Jenks & Sutor, *AXIOM: The Scientific Computation System*, Springer 1992, ISBN 3-540-97855-0) — the opposite pole from Maxima: a **strongly-typed** CAS with a rigorous category/domain hierarchy (Ring, Field, etc. as first-class types). The open Axiom project is a **literate program** distributed as ~15 numbered “volumes” (Vol. 0 Jenks & Sutor; Vol. 10.x the algebra implementation/theory/categories/domains/packages), freely downloadable from axiom-developer.org / nongnu.org/axiom. **This is essential reading for a Haskell implementer specifically**, because Axiom’s typed algebra hierarchy is the closest existing analog to what a type-driven Haskell CAS wants, and the literate volumes explain the *why*.
- **Singular, CoCoA, Macaulay2** (specialized: Gröbner bases / commutative algebra / algebraic geometry) and **PARI/GP** (number theory), **FLINT** (fast C library for number theory + polynomials, the modern high-performance substrate under Sage and others) — study these selectively when you need a specific algorithm done fast and correctly; FLINT in particular is the reference for state-of-the-art polynomial/bignum performance.
- **Symbolica** (Rust, with Python/C++ bindings; symbolica.io) — a modern, source-available high-performance CAS “built for large expressions.” Directly relevant for two reasons: (1) its pattern matcher explicitly supports commutative/associative matching and wildcards (`x_`) — a modern, fast take on Wolfram-style matching; (2) per Symbolica’s own writeup it provides “the only complete port of the latest Rubi 4.17 integration system outside the Wolfram Language, preserving its 7,000+ ordered rules and passing the complete 72,944-problem Rubi corpus” (in an MIT-licensed `symbolica-integrate` crate), processing that corpus “in 18 minutes of wall time, on a Ryzen 9 5900X parallelized over 8 cores” (57 ms median per problem). Lesson: rule-based integration (Rubi) as a pragmatic alternative/complement to Risch, and how to make AC-matching fast. (Note: Rubi’s own site, rulebasedintegration.org / Albert Rich’s Vision page, describes the ruleset as “over 6600–6700 integration rules”; the “7,000+” figure is Symbolica’s count of its port.)

### Pattern matching — the genuinely hard engineering

This is where a Wolfram-like engine lives or dies, and it is under-served by the CAS textbooks, so go to the rewriting/PM literature:

- **MatchPy** (Krebber, Barthels et al.; arXiv:1710.06915 and arXiv:1710.00077; open-source Python) — the best modern, readable treatment of “Mathematica-style” matching: syntactic matching plus **associative and/or commutative** functions, **sequence variables** (the BlankSequence/BlankNullSequence analogue), and constraints, with **many-to-one matching via discrimination nets**. The companion master’s thesis (arXiv:1705.00907, “Non-linear Associative-Commutative Many-to-One Pattern Matching with Sequence Variables”) is the most complete single document on the combined feature set. Key facts to internalize: non-syntactic (AC) matching is **NP-hard** in general (worst-case exponential), but discrimination nets amortize a fixed pattern set across many subjects, and linear (no repeated variables) AC matching is polynomial — hence the standard **two-stage** strategy (match the linearized pattern first, then check non-linear constraints).
- **AC-discrimination nets** (Bachmair, Chen, Ramakrishnan et al., ~1993; Springer LNCS) — the primary research on generalizing discrimination nets to associative-commutative symbols, and on term indexing for Knuth–Bendix completion. Read these before you optimize your matcher.
- For the Haskell angle, note there is **no Haskell library with Mathematica-grade matching** off the shelf; you will implement it. The `dumb-cas` Hackage package is an interesting, small Haskell take on “untyped symbolic rewriting with user-defined rules, Lisp-flexibility + regex-conciseness,” worth reading as a starting point even though it is minimal.

### Rewriting-based language design — what to learn from the neighbors

- **Maude** (SRI; rewriting logic, membership equational logic) — the gold standard for *efficient built-in matching modulo* associativity/commutativity/identity, with a high-performance C++ engine and powerful reflection/metaprogramming. **This is the system to study for how to implement AC-matching and equational rewriting efficiently and correctly.**
- **OBJ / OBJ3** (Goguen) — the order-sorted equational-logic ancestor of Maude; foundational for the “programming = equational specification + rewriting” idea.
- **ELAN, Stratego, Tom** — the *strategy* languages: they separate rewrite *rules* from the *strategies* that control their application. The Rascal paper’s observation is the key lesson: in practice “the strategies rather than the rewrite rules do the heavy lifting.” For a Wolfram-like system, this maps onto the distinction between your rule tables and your evaluation-control (`ReplaceRepeated`, `//.`, evaluation order, holding).
- **Rascal** (CWI) — meta-programming/language-workbench descendant of ASF+SDF; good for how to package rewriting into a usable language.
- **Pure** (Albert Gräf) — a modern, practical, *dynamically typed functional language based on term rewriting* with ML-style syntax; arguably the closest “small language built on term rewriting” to what the user is imagining, and readable.

### Expression representation, hash consing, memoization

- **Hash consing / structure sharing** is the central performance technique: intern structurally-equal subexpressions so equality is pointer comparison and identical subtrees are stored once. The MIT paper *“Efficient Symbolic Computation via Hash Consing”* (Bowen Zhu, arXiv:2509.20534) surveys the landscape: GiNaC uses reference counting; Symbolica uses a form of interning; the JuliaSymbolics work adds weak-reference hash-consing; and it notes that a Wolfram-internals blog post’s descriptions “suggest hash-consing is used internally,” while cautioning this is not confirmed with performance data. Key implementation caveat from that paper: hash-consing works best with **immutable** expressions and a tracing GC — which is precisely Haskell’s model, making Haskell unusually well-suited here. Design your `Expr` to be immutable and intern via a global (or `ST`/`IO`) hash table, or lean on Haskell’s sharing + a memo table for `simplify`.

### The Haskell-specific decisions

**Untyped vs. typed representation — the central call.** For the *Wolfram-style rewriting core*, use an **untyped single sum type**, e.g.
`data Expr = Int Integer | Rat Rational | Sym String | App Expr [Expr]` (everything is `f[args]`, mirroring Mathematica’s FullForm). This derives `Eq`/`Ord`/`Show` for free (needed for canonical ordering under Orderless and for memoization), supports uniform traversal/rewriting (via `uniplate` or `recursion-schemes`), and matches how every production Wolfram-like system (Maxima, Expreduce, Mathics) actually represents expressions. Christopher Olah’s **HaskSymb** experiment (github.com/colah/HaskSymb; blog post 2012) is a small, readable illustration of the untyped-plus-QuasiQuoter/ViewPatterns rewriting style — and his own conclusion is instructive: he found no clean way to put variables into types (“The big issue I’m facing is appropriate types for symbolic expressions. In particular, how do I handle variables in types?”) and calls the typed approach the unresolved crux. Take the hint: **do not try to make the core symbolic engine fully type-safe.**

Reserve **GADTs / type-level techniques for the algebra sub-libraries** (polynomials, finite fields, ring towers) where types buy real safety. Hiromi Ishii’s **“A Purely Functional Computer Algebra System Embedded in Haskell”** (arXiv:1807.01456; CASC 2018, LNCS 11077, pp. 288–303, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-99639-4_20) and his **`computational-algebra`** library (github.com/konn/computational-algebra) are the reference: they encode polynomial arity, monomial ordering, and coefficient ring as type-level parameters (using promoted naturals, singletons, and a Presburger type-checker plugin — his `type-natural` and `ghc-typelits-presburger` packages) so that elements of ℚ[x,y,z] and ℚ[w,x,y] cannot be added by mistake, and implement Gröbner bases with **F4, F5, and Hilbert-driven** algorithms.  Lesson: strong typing is worth it for the ring algebra; it needs heavy machinery and property-based testing (QuickCheck) because the type system checks arity/identity but **not** the ring *axioms*. Ishii positions this explicitly against **DoCon** (Mechveliani), the pioneering Haskell CAS, “with more emphasis on safety and correctness.”

**The algebraic-typeclass-hierarchy problem.** Haskell’s standard `Num` is inadequate for a CAS, for reasons the `numeric-prelude` documentation states plainly: it “defines no semantics for the fundamental operations” (e.g. nothing says addition is associative); it forces `Eq` and `Show` as superclasses (impossible to give non-bottom instances for, e.g., a function-valued ring like `data IntegerFunction a = IF (a -> Integer)`); and it lumps semantic operations together with representation-specific ones (`toInteger`, `decodeFloat`) and is “not fine-grained enough.” Two mature replacements:

- **`numeric-prelude`** (Thielemann et al.; Hackage) — an experimental alternative hierarchy splitting `Num` into `Additive`, `Ring`, `Field`, `Algebraic`, `Transcendental`, etc.  (mapping `Num → Additive, Ring, Absolute`; `Fractional → Field`; `Floating → Algebraic, Transcendental`; and so on). The HaskellWiki “Numeric Prelude” page and the package description are the canonical statement of *why* `Num` is wrong. Uses multi-parameter type classes; QuickCheck test suite. It candidly admits gaps (linear algebra, residue classes, fixed-point numbers).
- **`algebra`** (Edward Kmett; Hackage/github.com/ekmett/algebra) — a finer-grained hierarchy (magmas → semigroups → groups → rings → modules → algebras, with additive/multiplicative distinctions) that Ishii’s CAS builds on. `numhask` (Tony Day) is a cleaner modern alternative using `RebindableSyntax`.
  Use one of these as your coefficient tower rather than rolling your own or fighting `Num`.

**Extensibility — the expression problem.** If you want to add new expression-node kinds and new operations without editing existing code, the reference is Wouter Swierstra’s **“Data types à la carte”** (*JFP* 18(4):423–436, 2008, DOI 10.1017/S0956796808006758), which composes ASTs as coproducts of functors (`data (f :+: g) e = Inl (f e) | Inr (g e)`) tied together with `Fix`, plus a subtyping class `(:<:)` for injection; Phil Wadler called it the best Expression-Problem solution he’d seen in Haskell.  Matthew Pickering’s closed-type-family reimplementation and Patrick Bahr’s “Compositional Data Types” are the productionized descendants. In practice, for a Wolfram-like system where *everything is `f[args]` anyway*, you may not need à la carte at all — the untyped `Expr` sidesteps the expression problem by making the node set uniform. Use `recursion-schemes` (`cata`/`ana` over a base functor; Kmett’s library, or `data-fix`) or the `Free` monad (Chris Penner’s “ASTs with Fix and Free”; Bartosz Milewski’s higher-algebra posts) for clean folds/evaluators and for substitution.

**Other Haskell libraries worth reading as reference** (from the ecosystem survey): `poly` (Bodigrim) — fast `Vector`-backed uni/multivariate polynomials with Karatsuba multiplication,  slotting into the `semirings` `GcdDomain`/`Euclidean` classes (its maintainer benchmarks it “at least 20x–40x faster than the `polynomial` library” — a self-reported figure); `constructive-algebra` (Mörtberg et al.) — small, very readable constructive ring/ideal/matrix code (coherent rings without Noetherian assumptions); `sbv` (Erkök) — how to make symbolic values overload `Num`/`Ord` (`.==`, `.>`) and offload to an SMT solver like Z3 (useful for zero-testing side conditions, not for rewriting); `symengine` — thin FFI bindings if you want a fast external core; and the historical **DoCon** (Mechveliani), which used runtime “sample arguments” for ring identity — the design Ishii explicitly improves on. 

### Zero-testing and the undecidability wall

A conceptual point every implementer must internalize: **there is no general algorithm for deciding whether a symbolic expression is zero.** Richardson’s theorem — Daniel Richardson, “Some Unsolvable Problems Involving Elementary Functions of a Real Variable,” *Journal of Symbolic Logic* 33(4):514–520 (1968) — shows that for the class of expressions generated by the rational numbers, π, ln 2, the variable x, addition/subtraction/multiplication/composition, and the `sin`, `exp`, and `abs` functions, the predicate “E = 0?” is recursively undecidable. This is why every real CAS’s simplifier is a bundle of heuristics with a “good enough” contract rather than a decision procedure, why `Simplify` can’t be complete, and why canonical forms are only defined for restricted classes (polynomials, rational functions). Build your simplifier as a *confluent-as-possible, terminating* rewrite system with an explicit normal form for the sub-domains where one exists (rationals, polynomials), and treat general simplification as best-effort. (Baader & Nipkow give you the confluence/termination tools; the undecidability result tells you where the ceiling is.)

### University course materials and worked build-logs

Course materials that pair theory with build-it projects are scattered but real: von zur Gathen & Gerhard maintain a companion site for *Modern Computer Algebra* (cosec.bit.uni-bonn.de). The DIVA-portal MSc thesis “Constructing a Computer Algebra System Capable of…” (extending SymPy) and the Victor Brun “Building a Computer Algebra System in Go” blog series are good worked examples of scoping a from-scratch build. Fredrik Johansson’s (FLINT/Arb author) blog post “Things I would like to see in a computer algebra system” is a valuable practitioner’s list of what matters — including his point that “the core datatype in computer algebra is the arbitrary-size integer” while “hardware, compilers, programming languages are not at all designed for” it — a direct argument for getting your bignum/GMP substrate right first.

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Foundations (before writing the engine).** Read Cohen Vol. 1 chapters on expression trees and automatic simplification, and Baader & Nipkow chs. 1–4. Decide representation now: **untyped `Expr` sum type, immutable, with hash-consing**. Get a solid arbitrary-precision rational layer working first (Haskell’s `Integer` already uses GMP; add `Ratio`). Benchmark threshold to proceed: you can construct, hash-cons, and structurally compare large expressions cheaply.

**Stage 1 — Minimal viable CAS core.** Build the evaluator loop and matcher: (1) the fixed-point evaluate-until-unchanged loop; (2) attributes Flat, Orderless (with canonical sort), Listable, HoldAll/HoldFirst/HoldRest, OneIdentity; (3) pattern matching with Blank/BlankSequence/BlankNullSequence, conditional (`/;`) rules, and non-linear patterns; (4) DownValues/UpValues rule tables; (5) automatic simplification of `Plus`/`Times`/`Power` to a canonical form over rationals; (6) symbolic differentiation (the classic easy win). This is a genuine Wolfram-Language-subset kernel. **Study Expreduce’s source in parallel — it is your closest blueprint.** Benchmark threshold: your kernel reproduces the standard `D[...]` product/chain rules and sorts/flattens `a+b+a` → `2 a + b`.

**Stage 2 — Polynomial substrate.** Implement a real polynomial representation (start sparse-distributed for multivariate; add recursive/dense as needed), then GCD: Euclidean → subresultant PRS → modular/Brown’s (dense) and Zippel’s sparse interpolation for multivariate. This is where you *may* introduce Ishii-style typed polynomials as a separate, strongly-typed module. Follow Geddes/Czapor/Labahn and von zur Gathen & Gerhard. Benchmark: correct multivariate GCD and content/primitive-part on non-trivial inputs.

**Stage 3 — Factorization and the hard algorithms.** Factorization over finite fields (Berlekamp, Cantor–Zassenhaus) → Hensel lifting → Zassenhaus over ℤ → (optionally) van Hoeij/LLL. Then Gröbner bases (Buchberger first, then F4). Then integration: Hermite reduction + Rothstein–Trager for the rational part, then the transcendental Risch case from Bronstein; consider porting Rubi rules (as Symbolica/Symja did) as a pragmatic heuristic integrator alongside or before full Risch. This is the stage where **hobby projects stall** — sequence it last and accept partial coverage.

**Where projects typically die, and how to avoid it:** (a) trying to make the core type-safe and drowning in type-level machinery — *don’t; keep the core untyped*; (b) underestimating automatic simplification (the “boring” canonical-form logic is the actual hard part of feeling like a CAS) — *budget for it, and lean on Cohen*; (c) building integration/factorization before the polynomial/GCD/bignum substrate is solid — *invert the order*; (d) ignoring pattern-matcher performance until the rule set is large — *design discrimination-net/many-to-one matching in from Stage 1, informed by MatchPy*; (e) no memoization/structure-sharing — *hash-cons from day one*.

**Two-book minimum if budget-constrained:** Cohen Vol. 1 + Baader & Nipkow. **Free-PDF starting kit:** *A=B* (Wilf’s UPenn page), the Wolfram evaluation docs, the MatchPy and Ishii arXiv papers, the Axiom literate volumes, and the GiNaC/SymPy architecture papers.

## Caveats

- **“Read the source” varies wildly in payoff.** Expreduce and Mathics are small/readable and directly on-target; Maxima, Axiom, and Sage are huge and old, and reading them cover-to-cover is not a good use of time — grep them for specific algorithms.
- **Bronstein Volume II never appeared** (the author died); the algebraic-function integration case remains scattered across research papers, so “full Risch” for algebraic functions is genuinely frontier work, not a textbook exercise.
- **Karr’s summation algorithm** (difference fields) is not in *A=B*; you’ll need Karr’s original papers or Schneider’s Sigma work for that specific requirement.
- **Symbolica is source-available but commercially licensed** (free only for personal/non-commercial single-instance use) — fine to read and learn from, but check the license before depending on it; its Rubi-corpus and performance figures (72,944 problems in 18 minutes; “7,000+ rules”) are the vendor’s own reported benchmarks, and Rubi’s own site describes the ruleset as “over 6600–6700 rules.”
- **The claim that Wolfram uses hash-consing internally is inferred, not confirmed** — the Wolfram kernel is closed-source; treat internals writeups (including reverse-engineering blog posts and the community “Wolfram Language Specification” project at wltools.github.io) as informed inference, not authoritative.
- **Typed-CAS design is an open research problem.** Ishii’s and Axiom’s typed hierarchies are impressive but neither is a settled “right answer”; expect pragmatic compromises, and note that even Ishii verifies ring axioms only by property testing, not by proof.
- **Terese/Klop and the `numeric-prelude` primary pages** were not directly fetched within the research budget; those items lean on the subagent’s findings and secondary references and are worth a quick spot-check against the primary Hackage/Cambridge pages before you rely on exact edition/page details.
