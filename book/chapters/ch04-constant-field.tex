\chapter[The Constant Field]{The Constant Field and Absolute Irreducibility}\label{ch:constant-field}

Part~II assumes that the polynomial $F$ defining $E = K(x)[y]/(F)$ is
\emph{absolutely irreducible}\index{absolutely irreducible}: irreducible not only over $K(x)$ but over
$\Kbar(x)$. The assumption is used every time the constant field is enlarged,
which the logarithmic part forces whenever a residue is not in $K$: over the
larger field $L$ we want $L(x)[y]/(F)$ still to be a field. It is also needed
for the reduction modulo primes of Chapter~\ref{ch:finite-order}. This chapter
shows that the assumption is the same as saying that $K$ is the whole
constant field of $E$, and how to arrange it. It follows Chapter~3
of~\cite{trager1984}.

\section{The exact constant field}\label{sec:const-exact}

Let $K$ be a field of characteristic $0$ and $E$ a finite extension of
$K(x)$. The \emph{exact constant field} of $E$\index{exact constant field} is
\[
  K_0 = \{ c \in E : c \text{ is algebraic over } K \} ,
\]
the algebraic closure of $K$ in $E$. It is a field, and it is the field of
constants of the derivation $D$ of $E$ extending $d/dx$
\VolI{Lemma~3.3.2}. An example shows that it can be larger than $K$: in
$\Q(x)[y]/(y^4 - 2x^2)$ the element $y^2/x$ is a square root of $2$. Over
$\Q(\sqrt2)$ the polynomial $y^4 - 2x^2$ factors as $(y^2 - \sqrt2\, x)(y^2 +
\sqrt2\, x)$, and the field is $\Q(\sqrt2)(x)[y]/(y^2 - \sqrt2\, x)$, of degree
$2$ and not $4$ over its rational functions.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:const-degree}\standing{cited}{\cite[Lemmas~3.1.10 and~3.6.2]{stichtenoth2009}}
Let $L \supseteq K$ be fields with $K$ algebraically closed in $L$, and
$\alpha$ algebraic over $K$. Then $[L(\alpha) : L] = [K(\alpha) : K]$. In
particular $[M(x) : K(x)] = [M : K]$ for a finite extension $M$ of $K$.
\end{lemma}

The reason is that a factor over $L$ of the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$
over $K$ has coefficients that are algebraic over $K$, hence in $K$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:absolute-irreducibility}\standing{proved}{}
Let $F \in K[x][y]$ be irreducible over $K(x)$, and $E = K(x)[y]/(F)$. Then
$F$ is absolutely irreducible if and only if $K$ is algebraically closed in
$E$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$F$ is absolutely irreducible if and only if it is irreducible over $M(x)$
for every finite extension $M$ of $K$. Fix such an $M$, and let $E M$ be a
compositum of $E$ and $M$, generated over $M(x)$ by a root of an irreducible
factor of $F$. Counting degrees in two ways,
\[
  [E M : M(x)]\, [M(x) : K(x)] = [E M : E]\, [E : K(x)] ,
\]
and $[M(x) : K(x)] = [M : K]$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:const-degree}. So $F$ is
irreducible over $M(x)$, which means $[EM : M(x)] = [E : K(x)]$, if and only
if $[EM : E] = [M : K]$.

If $K$ is algebraically closed in $E$, write $M = K(\alpha)$;
Lemma~\ref{lem:const-degree} with $L = E$ gives $[EM : E] = [M : K]$.
Conversely, if $F$ is absolutely irreducible, take $M = K_0$; then $EM = E$,
so $[M : K] = 1$.
\end{proof}

This is Theorem~3.4 of~\cite{trager1984}, with his proof.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:exact-field}\standing{proved}{}
Let $F$ be irreducible over $K(x)$, $E = K(x)[y]/(F)$, and $K_0$ the exact
constant field of $E$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item the minimal polynomial $F_0$ of $y$ over $K_0(x)$ is absolutely
  irreducible, $E = K_0(x)[y]/(F_0)$, and $F_0$ is a factor of $F$ over
  $K_0(x)$;
\item $[K_0 : K]$ divides both $\deg_y(F)$ and $\deg_x(F)$; in particular $F$
  is absolutely irreducible if these two degrees are coprime.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
(i) $K_0$ is algebraically closed in $E = K_0(x)(y)$, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:absolute-irreducibility} applies over $K_0$. (ii) By the
degree count above with $M = K_0$, $[E : K(x)] = [K_0 : K]\, [E : K_0(x)]$,
and the same holds with the roles of $x$ and $y$ exchanged.
\end{proof}

So the assumption of Part~II is arranged by replacing $K$ by $K_0$ and $F$ by
$F_0$. The integrand is unchanged; only the field over which we say it is
defined has grown, and the degree of the curve has dropped by the factor $[K_0
: K]$, which makes every later step cheaper.

\section{Finding the absolutely irreducible factor}\label{sec:const-algorithm}

The difficulty is that $K_0$ is not known in advance, and one cannot factor
over $\Kbar$, which is not finitely generated. Trager's
observation~\cite[\S3.2]{trager1984} is that a field containing $K_0$ can be
read off a specialisation. Take $F$ monic in $y$, and $x_0 \in K$ such that
$F(x_0, y)$ is squarefree. Every element of $K_0$ is integral over $K[x]$,
and $x \mapsto x_0$ maps the integral closure of $K[x]$ in $E$ onto a ring
that has, for each irreducible factor $F_1$ of $F(x_0, y)$ over $K$, a
homomorphism onto the field $K[w]/(F_1(w))$. A homomorphism of fields is
injective, so $K_0$ embeds in each $K[w]/(F_1)$, and $F$ factors over
$K[w]/(F_1)$ at least as far as it does over $K_0$.

\begin{algo}{AbsoluteFactor}{F}{an absolutely irreducible factor}
\Spec{Given $F \in K[x][y]$, monic in $y$ and irreducible over $K(x)$, return
a finite extension $M$ of $K$ and a factor of $F$ over $M(x)$ that is
absolutely irreducible.}
\If{$\gcd(\deg_x(F), \deg_y(F)) = 1$} \Return $(K, F)$ \EndIf
\State $x_0 \gets$ an element of $K$ such that $F(x_0, y)$ is squarefree
\State $F_1 \gets$ an irreducible factor of $F(x_0, y)$ over $K$ of least degree
\If{$\deg(F_1) = 1$} \Return $(K, F)$ \EndIf
\State $M \gets K[w]/(F_1(w))$
\State \Return $M$ and an irreducible factor of $F$ over $M(x)$ of least degree
\end{algo}

This is the algorithm of~\cite[\S3.2]{trager1984}; its correctness is argued
there, and we have not reproduced the argument beyond the paragraph above.
The exact constant field is then generated over $K$ by the coefficients of
the factor returned. The last step needs factorisation of polynomials over an
algebraic number field, for which Trager refers to his own earlier algorithm.

\begin{example}\label{ex:absolute-factor}
Let $F = y^4 - 2x^2$ over $K = \Q$, the polynomial of
Section~\ref{sec:const-exact}. The degrees in $x$ and in $y$ are $2$ and $4$,
which are not coprime. Take $x_0 = 1$: $F(1, y) = y^4 - 2$ is squarefree, and
it is irreducible over $\Q$, so $F_1 = y^4 - 2$, of degree $4$, and $M =
\Q[w]/(w^4 - 2)$. Over $M(x)$,
\[
  y^4 - 2x^2 = (y^2 - w^2 x)(y^2 + w^2 x) ,
\]
and neither factor splits further, because $\pm w^2 x$ is not a square in
$M(x)$. The algorithm returns $M$ and $y^2 - w^2 x$. The coefficients of
that factor generate $\Q(w^2) = \Q(\sqrt2)$, which is the exact constant
field found in Section~\ref{sec:const-exact}. The field $M$ is larger: the
specialisation gives a field that contains $K_0$, not $K_0$ itself.
\end{example}

Trager adds a test that often makes the algorithm unnecessary. If $w$ is an
integral basis of $E$ normal at infinity, the elements of $E$ with no poles
at all are spanned over $K$ by the $w_i$ that have no pole at infinity
(the first part of Lemma~\ref{lem:termwise}, which does not need $K$ to be
algebraically closed in $E$), and these elements are exactly $K_0$. So $F$ is
absolutely irreducible if and only if exactly one $w_i$ is integral at
infinity. Since the integration algorithm computes such a basis anyway, the
test is free. In the example above, $(1, y, y^2/x, y^3/x)$ is an integral
basis of $\Q(x)[y]/(y^4 - 2x^2)$, and two of its elements, $1$ and $y^2/x$,
have no poles.

Later algorithms for absolute factorisation are faster and are not needed
for what follows. Kaltofen~\cite{kaltofen1985} gave a test for absolute
irreducibility; Duval~\cite{duval1991} factors by counting the components of
the curve through geometric invariants; and Ch\`eze and
Lecerf~\cite{cheze2007} give a probabilistic algorithm\later\ whose cost is
less than quadratic in the dense size of the polynomial. We have read only
the abstracts and introductions of these three.

\section{Binomial polynomials}\label{sec:const-binomial}

For $F = y^n - g(x)$ the question has a closed answer.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:binomial}\standing{proved}{}
Let $g \in K[x]$ be nonconstant, with squarefree factorisation $g = c
\prod_i g_i^{e_i}$, $c \in K^*$, the $g_i$ monic, squarefree, pairwise coprime
and nonconstant. Let $n \ge 2$ and $\delta = \gcd(n, e_1, e_2, \dots)$. Then
$y^n - g$ is absolutely irreducible if and only if $\delta = 1$. In general,
\[
  y^{n/\delta} - c^{1/\delta} \prod_i g_i^{e_i/\delta}
\]
is an absolutely irreducible factor of $y^n - g$ over $K(c^{1/\delta})(x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We use the criterion quoted in~\cite[Theorem~3.6]{trager1984}: for a field
$k$ and $a \in k^*$, the polynomial $X^n - a$ is irreducible over $k$ provided
that $a$ is not a $p$-th power in $k$ for any prime $p$ dividing $n$, and, if
$4$ divides $n$, that $a \notin -4 k^4$. Apply it with $k = \Kbar(x)$ and $a
= g$. Since $\Kbar$ is algebraically closed and $\Kbar[x]$ is a unique
factorisation domain, $g$ is a $p$-th power in $\Kbar(x)$ exactly when $p$
divides every $e_i$; and $-4$ is a fourth power in $\Kbar$, so $g \in -4
k^4$ exactly when $4$ divides every $e_i$. If $\delta = 1$, no prime dividing
$n$ divides every $e_i$, and both conditions hold. If $\delta > 1$, then $g =
h^\delta$ with $h = c^{1/\delta} \prod_i g_i^{e_i/\delta}$, and $y^{n/\delta}
- h$ divides $y^n - h^\delta$; it is absolutely irreducible by the first
part, because $\gcd(n/\delta, e_1/\delta, \dots) = 1$, or trivially if $n =
\delta$.
\end{proof}

This is the algorithm of~\cite[\S3.3]{trager1984}. For the normal form $z^n
= H$ of~\eqref{eq:radical-normal}, the exponents are those $j$ with $p_j \ne
1$. For a square root $y^2 = p$ with $p$ squarefree and nonconstant, $\delta
= 1$: the curves $y^2 = 1 - x^3$ and $y^2 = x^8 + 1$ are absolutely
irreducible. When the radicand is constant, $y^n = c$, the extension is an
extension of the constant field and not a curve at all.
