\chapter{Genus Zero}\label{ch:genus-zero}

When the curve has genus $0$, none of the difficulties of
Chapters~\ref{ch:algebraic-part} to~\ref{ch:finite-order} arise. The function
field is, after at most a quadratic extension of the constants, a field of
rational functions $K(u)$ in a single parameter $u$; an algebraic integrand
becomes a rational function of $u$; and Volume~I integrates it. Every such
integral is elementary. This is the case that most integrands with a square
root of a quadratic fall into, and the classical substitutions for them are
instances of it.

The chapter shows how to recognise genus $0$, how to compute a parameter
with the tools of Chapters~\ref{ch:integral-bases} and~\ref{ch:divisors},
and how the general algorithm degenerates. The setting is that of Part~II.

\section{Riemann--Roch spaces}\label{sec:g0-rr}

The principality test of Theorem~\ref{thm:principal} computes more than it
was asked for.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:rr-space}\standing{proved}{}
Let $A$ be a finite divisor defined over $K$, and let $b = (b_1, \dots,
b_n)$ be a $K[x]$-basis of the fractional ideal $I(-A)$ that is normal at
infinity, with $\tau_i$ as in~\eqref{eq:tau}. Then the elements of $E$ in
$\LL(A)$ are
\[
  \Bigl\{ \sum_{i=1}^n p_i b_i : p_i \in K[x],\ p_i = 0 \text{ or } \deg(p_i) \le -\tau_i \Bigr\} ,
\]
a vector space over $K$ of dimension $\sum_{\tau_i \le 0} (1 - \tau_i)$, with
basis the $x^j b_i$ for $0 \le j \le -\tau_i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
An element $u \in E$ is in $\LL(A)$ when $\ord{P}(u) \ge -n_P$ at the finite
places, that is, $u \in I(-A)$, and $u$ has no pole at infinity, $A$ having no
infinite place in its support. Write $u = \sum_i p_i b_i$ with $p_i \in K[x]$
and apply Lemma~\ref{lem:termwise}.
\end{proof}

For a divisor with support at infinity one first multiplies by a power of
$x$; the general case is Hess's~\cite{hess2002}. With $A = 0$ the proposition
recovers the constants, and for $\deg(A) = 0$ it is, applied to $-A$,
Theorem~\ref{thm:principal}.

\section{Recognising genus zero and finding a parameter}\label{sec:g0-parameter}

By Corollary~\ref{cor:genus-tau}, if $w$ is an integral basis normal at
infinity then
\[
  g = 0 \iff \tau_1 + \dots + \tau_n = n - 1 .
\]
For $y^2 = p(x)$ with $p$ squarefree this is $\lceil \deg(p)/2 \rceil = 1$:
the conics.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:g0-parameter}\standing{proved}{}
Let $E$ have genus $0$, and suppose that $E$ has a place $P$ of degree $1$.
Then $\ell(P) = 2$, and for any $u \in \LL(P) \setminus K$ we have $E =
K(u)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:riemann-roch}(iii), $\ell(P) = \deg(P) + 1 - g = 2$, the
dimension being the same over $K$ as over $\Kbar$
\cite[Theorem~3.6.3(d)]{stichtenoth2009}. A nonconstant $u \in \LL(P)$ has
exactly one pole, a simple one at $P$, so $[E : K(u)] = 1$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:riemann-roch}(i). This is
\cite[Proposition~1.6.3]{stichtenoth2009}.
\end{proof}

A nonsingular point $(x_0, y_0)$ of the curve $F = 0$ with coordinates in
$K$ carries exactly one place, which has degree $1$ and is given by the ideal
$\OO (x - x_0) + \OO (y - y_0)$. (At a singular point there may be several
places, or none of degree $1$.)
Proposition~\ref{prop:rr-space} with $A = P$ then gives $u$.

\begin{algo}{RationalParameter}{P}{a parameter on a curve of genus zero}
\Spec{Given a finite place $P$ of degree $1$ of a function field $E$ of
genus $0$, return $u$ with $E = K(u)$ and the rational functions $X, Y$ with
$x = X(u)$, $y = Y(u)$.}
\State $I \gets$ a $K[x]$-basis of the inverse of the ideal of $P$ \Comment{Section~\ref{sec:ib-criterion}}
\State $(b, \tau) \gets \alg{NormalizeAtInfinity}(I, v)$ \Comment{$v$ a local integral basis at infinity}
\State $u \gets$ an element of the space of Proposition~\ref{prop:rr-space} not in $K$
\State $(X, Y) \gets$ the solution of $F(x, y) = 0$, $u = u(x, y)$ for $x$ and $y$ as rational functions of $u$
\State \Return $(u, X, Y)$
\end{algo}

The last step is elimination: $x$ and $y$ are elements of $E = K(u)$, and
each is the root of a polynomial of degree $1$ over $K(u)$ obtained from a
resultant.

\begin{example}\label{ex:g0-conic}
Let $E = \Q(x)[y]/(y^2 - x^2 - 1)$. The integral basis $(1, y)$ has $\tau =
(0, 1)$, so $g = 0 + 1 - 2 + 1 = 0$. Take $P = (0, 1)$, and let $P' = (0,
-1)$. Since $(x) = P + P' - \infty_1 - \infty_2$, the inverse of the ideal of
$P$ is $x^{-1}$ times the ideal of $P'$, which consists of the $a + b\,y$ with
$a(0) = b(0)$ and has basis $(x, y + 1)$. So
\[
  b = \Bigl( 1,\; \frac{y + 1}{x} \Bigr),
  \qquad M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1/x & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\]
with respect to the local integral basis $v = (1, y/x)$ at infinity. Here
$\kappa = (0, 0)$ and $N$ is the identity, so $b$ is normal at infinity with
$\tau = (0, 0)$, and $\LL(P)$ has basis $1$ and
\[
  u = \frac{y + 1}{x} .
\]
Substituting $y = u x - 1$ in $y^2 = x^2 + 1$ gives $x\,(u^2 - 1) = 2u$, so
\[
  x = \frac{2u}{u^2 - 1}, \qquad y = \frac{u^2 + 1}{u^2 - 1} .
\]
Geometrically, $u$ is the slope of the line through $P'$ and the point $(x,
y)$.
\end{example}

\paragraph{When no rational point is known.} A curve of genus $0$ over $K$
need not have a point with coordinates in $K$: $x^2 + y^2 = -1$ over $\Q$
has none. Van Hoeij~\cite{vanhoeij1997} avoids choosing a point. He takes
for $A$ the negative of a canonical divisor, which is defined over $K$ and
has degree $2$; then $\ell(A) = 3$, and a basis $(g_1, g_2, g_3)$ of $\LL(A)$,
computed from an integral basis, maps the curve bijectively onto a conic. A
conic has a point over an extension of $K$ of degree at most $2$, found by
intersecting it with a line, and a point gives a parametrisation. When the
degree of the curve is odd, or any divisor of odd degree defined over $K$ is
known, he constructs a rational point on the conic, and the parametrisation
needs no extension of $K$ at all. We have read the abstract and introduction
of his paper and have not reproduced the algorithm. The book of Sendra,
Winkler and P\'erez-D\'iaz~\cite{sendra2008} treats the same problem by
adjoint curves; of it we have read only the table of contents.

\section{Integration}\label{sec:g0-integration}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:g0-integration}\standing{proved}{}
Let $E$ have genus $0$, and let $E_L = L(u)$ for an algebraic extension $L$
of $K$ and a parameter $u$, with $x = X(u)$. Then for $f \in E$, written as a
rational function $\phi(u)$ of $u$,
\[
  \int f\,dx = \int \phi(u)\, X'(u)\, du ,
\]
which is the integral of a rational function. In particular every $f \in E$
has an elementary integral.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$dx = X'(u)\,du$, and a rational function has an elementary integral
\VolI{Chapter~2}.
\end{proof}

\begin{algo}{IntegrateGenusZero}{f}{integration on a curve of genus zero}
\Spec{Given $f \in E$, where $E$ has genus $0$, return an elementary integral
of $f$.}
\State $(u, X, Y) \gets$ a parameter of $E$, over an extension of $K$ of degree at most $2$
\State $\phi \gets f(X(u), Y(u)) \cdot X'(u)$
\State $G \gets \alg{IntegrateRationalFunction}(\phi)$ \Comment{Volume~I, \S2.5}
\State \Return $G$ with $u$ replaced by $u(x, y)$
\end{algo}

Seen from the general algorithm, genus $0$ is the case in which every
residue divisor is principal with $n_j = 1$ and there are no differentials of
the first kind, so that \seealg{LogarithmicPart} cannot fail
(Section~\ref{sec:log-divisors}). The parametrisation does the same work
without ideals, and it is what an implementation should use: Axiom's
integrator, for one, has a separate package for curves of genus $0$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:g0-sqrt2}
We integrate again the function of Example~\ref{ex:log-sqrt2},
\[
  \int \frac{dx}{(x - 1)\sqrt{x^2 + 1}} .
\]
With the parameter of Example~\ref{ex:g0-conic},
\[
  x - 1 = -\frac{u^2 - 2u - 1}{u^2 - 1}, \qquad
  \frac{dx}{y} = -\frac{2\,du}{u^2 - 1} ,
\]
so the integral is
\[
  \int \frac{2\,du}{u^2 - 2u - 1}
  = \frac{1}{\sqrt2} \log \frac{u - 1 - \sqrt2}{u - 1 + \sqrt2},
  \qquad u = \frac{\sqrt{x^2 + 1} + 1}{x} .
\]
The Rothstein--Trager resultant of $2/(u^2 - 2u - 1)$ is, up to a constant,
$2Z^2 - 1$, the residue polynomial found in Example~\ref{ex:log-sqrt2}. The
two answers differ by a constant.
\end{example}

\section{The classical substitutions}\label{sec:g0-euler}

For $y^2 = a x^2 + b x + c$ the textbooks give three substitutions, named
after Euler\index{Euler substitutions}, that rationalise integrals of $R(x, y)$:
\[
  y = \pm\sqrt a\, x + u, \qquad
  y = u\, x \pm \sqrt c, \qquad
  y = u\,(x - x_1) ,
\]
the first when $a$ is a square, the second when $c$ is, and the third when
$x_1$ is a root of the quadratic. Each is the parameter of
Theorem~\ref{thm:g0-parameter} for a particular place $P$: in the first, $u =
y \mp \sqrt a\, x$ has its only pole at one of the two places at infinity; in
the second, $u = (y \mp \sqrt c)/x$ is the slope from the point $(0, \pm\sqrt
c)$, as in Example~\ref{ex:g0-conic}; in the third, $u$ is the slope from
the branch point $(x_1, 0)$. The three conditions are the conditions for
those places to have degree $1$ over $K$. Over $\Kbar$ all three are always
available; over $K$ at least one is available after an extension of degree
at most $2$.

The three integrable cases of the binomial differential
(Theorem~\ref{thm:chebyshev}) are of the same kind. After $t = x^r$ the
differential lives on the curve $w^N = a + b t$, or $w^N = t$, or $w^N = (a
+ b t)/t$, each of which is a line with coordinate $w$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:g0-binomial}
For $\int x^5 (1 + x^3)^{2/3}\,dx$ we have $(p + 1)/r = 2$. With $t = x^3$
and $w = (1 + t)^{1/3}$, so that $t = w^3 - 1$ and $dt = 3w^2\,dw$,
\[
  \int x^5 (1 + x^3)^{2/3}\,dx = \frac13 \int t\, w^2\, dt
  = \int (w^7 - w^4)\, dw
  = \frac{(1 + x^3)^{8/3}}{8} - \frac{(1 + x^3)^{5/3}}{5} .
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:g0-pseudo}
The integral of Example~\ref{ex:running-log} was found on an elliptic curve,
but it is a binomial differential with $(p + 1)/r = 0$, and the substitution
$t = x^3$, $w = \sqrt{1 - t}$ gives
\[
  \int \frac{dx}{x\sqrt{1 - x^3}} = \frac13 \int \frac{dt}{t\, w}
  = \frac13 \int \frac{2\,dw}{w^2 - 1}
  = \frac13 \log \frac{w - 1}{w + 1} ,
\]
the same answer. The differential on $y^2 = 1 - x^3$ is the pull-back of a
differential on the conic $w^2 = 1 - t$ under $(x, y) \mapsto (x^3, y)$. This
is a common reason for an elliptic integral to be pseudo-elliptic, and it
is what the heuristics of Section~\ref{sec:radical-later} look for. The
algorithm of Chapters~\ref{ch:algebraic-part}
to~\ref{ch:finite-order} finds the integral without finding the map.
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}
Use \seealg{RationalParameter} with the place at $(1, 0)$ to parametrise $y^2
= 1 - x^2$, and integrate $1/\sqrt{1 - x^2}$. Which Euler substitution is
this?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that $y^3 = x^2 (x - 1)$ has genus $0$, using the result of the first
exercise of Chapter~\ref{ch:radical}, and find a parameter.
\end{exercise}

Show that the curve $x^2 + y^2 = -1$ has genus $0$ and no place of degree $1$
over $\Q$, and parametrise it over $\Q(i)$.
\end{exercise}
