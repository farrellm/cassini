\chapter{Integral Bases}\label{ch:integral-bases}

An \emph{integral basis} of $E$ over $k(t)$ is a basis $w = (w_1, \dots,
w_n)$ such that $\OO_w = \OO$: the integral elements of $E$ are exactly the
combinations of the $w_i$ with polynomial coefficients. One exists by
Theorem~\ref{thm:closure-fg}. With respect to an integral basis the
denominator of an element (Definition~\ref{def:numden}) records its finite
poles faithfully, and that is what the Hermite reduction needs: over a basis
of integral elements that is not an integral basis, a function can have a
denominator without having a pole, and the reduction step can fail
(Example~\ref{ex:fail}).

This chapter gives Trager's algorithm for computing an integral
basis~\cite[Chapter~2]{trager1984}, the companion notion at infinity and the
procedure that makes an integral basis normal there, and the formula that
reads the genus off the result. Notation is that of
Chapter~\ref{ch:preliminaries}; from Section~\ref{sec:ib-infinity} on, $k = K$
is a field of constants and $t = x$.

\section{Trager's criterion}\label{sec:ib-criterion}

Let $V \subseteq \OO$ be a subring containing $k[t]$ that is a free
$k[t]$-module of rank $n$; for instance $V = k[t][y]$ for an integral
primitive element $y$, or $V = \OO_w$ for a basis $w$ such that $\OO_w$ is
closed under multiplication. For a nonzero ideal $\mathfrak m$ of $V$, the
\emph{idealizer} of $\mathfrak m$ is
\[
  \operatorname{Id}(\mathfrak m) = \{ u \in E : u\, \mathfrak m \subseteq \mathfrak m \} .
\]
It is a ring containing $V$, and it is contained in $\OO$: if $m_1, \dots,
m_n$ generate $\mathfrak m$ over $k[t]$ and $u m_i = \sum_j r_{ij} m_j$ with
$r_{ij} \in k[t]$, then $\det(r_{ij} - u\, \delta_{ij})$ annihilates
$\mathfrak m$, hence is zero, and it is a monic polynomial equation for $u$
over $k[t]$~\cite[Lemma~2.2]{trager1984}. So an idealizer larger than $V$ is a
larger ring of integral elements. The point of the criterion is the
converse.

For $q \in k[t]$ squarefree, the \emph{$q$-radical} of $V$ is
\[
  J_q(V) = \{ u \in V : u^N \in q V \text{ for some } N \ge 1 \} ,
\]
the intersection of the prime ideals of $V$ that contain $q$.

\begin{theorem}[Trager]\label{thm:trager-criterion}\standing{cited}{\cite[Theorem~2.1, Corollary~2.8, Theorem~2.10]{trager1984}}
Let $V$ be as above, with $k[t]$-basis $v$, and let $q$ be the product of the
monic irreducible $p \in k[t]$ such that $p^2$ divides $\disc(v)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $V = \OO$ if and only if $\operatorname{Id}(J_q(V)) = V$.
\item $J_q(V) = \{ u \in V : q \text{ divides } \operatorname{Tr}(u w) \text{
  for all } w \in V \}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Part~(i) comes from the theory of Dedekind domains: $V$ is integrally closed
if and only if all its nonzero prime ideals are invertible; a prime that
does not contain the discriminant is invertible; and for a prime $\mathfrak
m$ that is not invertible, $\mathfrak m^{-1} = \operatorname{Id}(\mathfrak m)
\supsetneq V$. Primes over an irreducible $p$ with $p^2 \nmid \disc(v)$ need
not be examined, by Corollary~\ref{cor:disc-drops}. Part~(ii) says that the
radical equals the \emph{trace radical}; it holds because the characteristic
of $k[t]/(p)$ is $0$, by Newton's identities relating the traces of the
powers of $u$ to the coefficients of its minimal polynomial. Trager took the
method from the algorithm of Zassenhaus~\cite{zassenhaus1972} and
Ford~\cite{ford1978} for the ring of integers of a number field, where small
primes need a different treatment of the radical.

Both parts are effective.

\emph{The radical.} Let $T = (\operatorname{Tr}(v_i v_j))$ be the trace
matrix of $v$. By~(ii), $u = \sum_i u_i v_i$ with $u_i \in k[t]$ lies in
$J_q(V)$ if and only if $T u \in q\, k[t]^n$. So $J_q(V)$ is the set of $u \in
k(t)^n$ such that $\binom{T}{q I_n} u \in q\, k[t]^{2n}$, and a basis of it is
obtained by row reduction as described at the end of
Section~\ref{sec:prelim-hermite}.

\emph{The idealizer.} Let $m = (m_1, \dots, m_n)$ be a $k[t]$-basis of
$\mathfrak m$, and for each $i$ let $M_i$ be the matrix over $k(t)$ of the
map $u \mapsto u\, m_i$, taking coordinates with respect to $v$ to
coordinates with respect to $m$. Then $u \in \operatorname{Id}(\mathfrak m)$
if and only if $M_i u \in k[t]^n$ for every $i$. Stacking the $M_i$ gives an
$n^2 \times n$ matrix; row reduction leaves a square matrix $\tilde M$, and
the columns of $\tilde M^{-1}$ are the coordinates with respect to $v$ of a
basis of $\operatorname{Id}(\mathfrak m)$~\cite[\S2.2]{trager1984}. With
coordinates of the output taken with respect to $v$ instead of $m$, the same
procedure computes the inverse ideal $\mathfrak m^{-1} = \{ u : u\, \mathfrak
m \subseteq V \}$, which Chapter~\ref{ch:divisors} uses.

\begin{algo}{IntegralBasis}{F}{Trager's algorithm}
\Spec{Given $F \in k[t][y]$, monic in $y$ and irreducible of degree $n$,
return an integral basis of $E = k(t)[y]/(F)$ over $k(t)$.}
\State $v \gets (1, y, \dots, y^{n-1})$, \ $d \gets$ the discriminant of $F$
\Loop
  \State $q \gets$ the product of the monic irreducible $p$ with $p^2 \mid d$
  \If{$q = 1$} \Return $v$ \EndIf
  \State $m \gets$ a basis of the $q$-radical of $\OO_v$, from the trace matrix of $v$
  \State $\bar v \gets$ a basis of the idealizer of that radical
  \State $A \gets$ the matrix with $v_i = \sum_j A_{ij} \bar v_j$
  \If{$\det(A) \in k$} \Return $v$ \EndIf
  \State $d \gets d / \det(A)^2$, \ $v \gets \bar v$
\EndLoop
\end{algo}

The loop is executed at most $\frac12 \deg(d) + 1$ times, by
Corollary~\ref{cor:disc-drops}, and the result is an integral basis by
Theorem~\ref{thm:trager-criterion}. The product $q$ is found from a
squarefree factorisation of $d$, without factoring into irreducibles. Trager
notes one more economy: after the first pass, only the factors of $q$ at
which the last idealizer was larger need be kept.

\begin{example}\label{ex:ib-cusp}
Let $F = y^2 - x^3$. Then $v = (1, y)$, the trace matrix is
$\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2x^3 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and $d
= 4x^3$, so $q = x$. An element $u_1 + u_2\, y$ is in the $x$-radical when $x$
divides $2u_1$ and $2x^3 u_2$, that is, when $x$ divides $u_1$: the radical
has basis $m = (x, y)$. For $u = a + b\,y$ with $a, b \in k(x)$,
\[
  u \cdot x = (a)\, x + (b x)\, y, \qquad
  u \cdot y = (b x^2)\, x + (a)\, y ,
\]
so $u$ is in the idealizer when $a$, $bx$ and $b x^2$ are polynomials: the
idealizer has basis $\bar v = (1, y/x)$. Then $\det(A) = x$, and $d$ becomes
$4x$. On the second pass $q = 1$, and the algorithm returns $(1, y/x)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:ib-quartic}
Let $F = y^4 + (x^2 + x)\,y - x^2$, the curve of Example~\ref{ex:fail}. The
trace matrix of $v = (1, y, y^2, y^3)$ is
\[
  T = \begin{pmatrix}
    4 & 0 & 0 & -3x(x+1) \\
    0 & 0 & -3x(x+1) & 4x^2 \\
    0 & -3x(x+1) & 4x^2 & 0 \\
    -3x(x+1) & 4x^2 & 0 & 3x^2(x+1)^2
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
with determinant $d = -x^4 (27x^4 + 108x^3 + 418x^2 + 108x + 27)$, so $q = x$.
Modulo $x$ only the entry $4$ survives: the $x$-radical consists of the $u$
with $x \mid u_1$, and has basis $m = (x, y, y^2, y^3)$. Its idealizer
contains $y^3/x$, because
\[
  \frac{y^3}{x} \cdot y = x - (x + 1)\, y, \qquad
  \frac{y^3}{x} \cdot y^2 = x\,y - (x + 1)\, y^2, \qquad
  \frac{y^3}{x} \cdot y^3 = x\,y^2 - (x + 1)\, y^3
\]
all lie in the radical, and solving the linear conditions shows that it is
$\OO_{\bar v}$ with $\bar v = (1, y, y^2, y^3/x)$. So $\det(A) = x$ and $d$
becomes $-x^2 (27x^4 + \dots + 27)$.

On the second pass $q = x$ again. The trace matrix of $\bar v$ modulo $x$ is
\[
  \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 & 0 & -3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix},
\]
so the radical is $\{ u : x \mid u_1,\ x \mid u_4 \}$, with basis $(x, y, y^2,
y^3)$ once more, now inside $\OO_{\bar v}$. This time the linear conditions
for $(c_1 + c_2\, y + c_3\, y^2 + c_4\, y^3/x)/x$ to lie in the idealizer
have only the zero solution modulo $x$: the idealizer is $\OO_{\bar v}$
itself, $\det(A) = 1$, and the algorithm returns
\[
  \Bigl( 1,\; y,\; y^2,\; \frac{y^3}{x} \Bigr) .
\]
This is the basis that the lazy Hermite reduction arrived at in
Example~\ref{ex:fail-extend}, and it is now shown to be an integral basis.
\end{example}

\section{Other algorithms}\label{sec:ib-others}

Van Hoeij~\cite{vanhoeij1994} computes an integral basis from the Puiseux
expansions at the points over the multiple factors of the discriminant, ``a
variant on the Coates' algorithm'' in his words; his paper says that Trager's
method was the one then used by Maple and Axiom. The general problem, the
integral closure of a Noetherian ring, has a criterion due to Grauert and
Remmert, made into an algorithm by de~Jong~\cite{dejong1998}, to which the
idealizer test above is closely related; later work on it\later\ is beyond
what a curve needs. We have not used any of these, and the integration
algorithm does not care which method produced the basis.

\section{Bases normal at infinity}\label{sec:ib-infinity}

From here on $k = K$ and $t = x$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:places}(iii), the ring
$\OO_\infty$ of elements of $E$ without poles at infinity is a free module of
rank $n$ over $K(x)_\infty$. A basis of that module is a \emph{local integral
basis at infinity}. One is computed by the substitution $z = 1/x$, which
turns infinity into the point $z = 0$: the algorithm of
Section~\ref{sec:ib-criterion}, run for $E$ over $K(z)$ with $q = z$
throughout and the other factors of the discriminant ignored, gives a basis
of the integral closure of the local ring of $K(z)$ at $z = 0$, which is
$K(x)_\infty$~\cite[\S2.3]{trager1984}.

An integral basis and a local integral basis at infinity are in general
different bases. A basis that is \emph{normal at infinity}
(Definition~\ref{def:normal-infinity}) serves both purposes at once, up to
powers of $x$. The following procedure makes a basis of any $K[x]$-module
normal at infinity without changing the module; it is applied to $\OO$ here
and to ideals in Chapter~\ref{ch:divisors}.

\begin{algo}{NormalizeAtInfinity}{b, v}{make a module basis normal at infinity}
\Spec{Given a basis $b$ of $E$ over $K(x)$ and a local integral basis $v$ at
infinity, return a basis $\bar b$ of $E$ that is normal at infinity and
generates the same $K[x]$-module as $b$, with the integers $\tau_i$
of~\eqref{eq:tau}.}
\Loop
  \State $M \gets$ the matrix over $K(x)$ with $b_i = \sum_j M_{ij} v_j$
  \For{$i \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $n$}
    \State $\kappa_i \gets \max_j \deg(M_{ij})$
  \EndFor
  \State $N \gets$ the matrix over $K$ with $N_{ij}$ the value at infinity of $x^{-\kappa_i} M_{ij}$
  \If{$\det(N) \ne 0$} \Return $(b, \kappa)$ \EndIf
  \State $c \gets$ a nonzero vector in $K^n$ with $c\, N = 0$
  \State $i_0 \gets$ an index with $c_{i_0} \ne 0$ and $\kappa_{i_0}$ greatest among such
  \State $b_{i_0} \gets \sum_i c_i\, x^{\kappa_{i_0} - \kappa_i}\, b_i$ \Comment{sum over $i$ with $c_i \ne 0$}
\EndLoop
\end{algo}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:normalize}\standing{proved}{}
\seealg{NormalizeAtInfinity} terminates and is correct, with $\tau_i =
\kappa_i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The entries of $x^{-\kappa_i} M_{ij}$ are in $K(x)_\infty$, so $x^{-\kappa_i}
b_i \in \OO_\infty$, and the matrix expressing these elements in the basis $v$
has entries in $K(x)_\infty$ and determinant with value $\det(N)$ at infinity.
If $\det(N) \ne 0$, that matrix is invertible over $K(x)_\infty$, so $(x^{-
\kappa_i} b_i)$ is a basis of $\OO_\infty$: the basis $b$ is normal at
infinity with $\tau_i = \kappa_i$.

Otherwise, the new $b_{i_0}$ is a combination of the $b_i$ with polynomial
coefficients in which $b_{i_0}$ itself has the coefficient $c_{i_0} \in K^*$,
so the module is unchanged. Its row in $M$ is $\sum_i c_i x^{\kappa_{i_0} -
\kappa_i} M_i$, whose entries have degree at most $\kappa_{i_0}$, with
coefficients of $x^{\kappa_{i_0}}$ given by $c\,N = 0$. So $\kappa_{i_0}$
strictly decreases and the other $\kappa_i$ are unchanged. But $\sum_i
\kappa_i \ge \deg(\det M)$, and $\det(M)$ changes only by a factor in $K^*$.
So the loop is executed finitely many times.
\end{proof}

This is the procedure of~\cite[\S2.3]{trager1984}, where $-\kappa_i$ is
called the representation order of $b_i$. An example in which the loop is
entered is Example~\ref{ex:principal-running} below; for an integral basis
of a simple radical extension it never is
(Theorem~\ref{thm:radical-basis}).

\section{The genus}\label{sec:ib-genus}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:genus}\standing{cited}{\cite[\S2.4]{trager1984}; \cite[Theorems~3.4.13 and~3.5.1]{stichtenoth2009}}
Suppose that $K$ is algebraically closed in $E$. Let $w$ be an integral basis
of $E$ over $K(x)$ and $v$ a local integral basis at infinity. Then the genus
of $E$ is
\[
  g = \tfrac12 \bigl( \deg(\disc(w)) - \deg(\disc(v)) \bigr) - n + 1 .
\]
\end{theorem}

Here $-\deg(\disc(v))$ is the order at infinity of the discriminant of $v$.
The formula is the Hurwitz genus formula for $E$ over $K(x)$: the two
discriminants measure the ramification at the finite places and at
infinity, which in characteristic $0$ is $\sum_P (r_P - 1)$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:genus-tau}\standing{proved}{}
If $w$ is an integral basis of $E$ over $K(x)$ that is normal at infinity,
with $\tau_i$ as in~\eqref{eq:tau}, and $K$ is algebraically closed in $E$,
then
\[
  g = \tau_1 + \dots + \tau_n - n + 1 .
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $v = (x^{-\tau_i} w_i)$. It is a local integral basis at infinity, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:disc}(ii) gives
\[
  \disc(v) = x^{-2 (\tau_1 + \dots + \tau_n)}\, \disc(w) . \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:genus}
For $y^2 = p(x)$ with $p$ squarefree of degree $m$, the basis $(1, y)$ has
$\tau = (0, \lceil m/2 \rceil)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:radical-basis}, so $g =
\lceil m/2 \rceil - 1$: genus $0$ for $m \le 2$, $1$ for $m = 3, 4$, and $3$
for $m = 8$.

For the quartic of Example~\ref{ex:ib-quartic}, the Newton polygon of $F$ at
infinity has two sides: $y^4 \approx -x^2 y$ gives three conjugate expansions
$y \approx c\,x^{2/3}$ with $c^3 = -1$, and $x^2 y \approx x^2$ gives one
expansion with $y \approx 1$. So there are two places at infinity: $P_1$
with $r = 3$, at which $y$ has order $-2$, and $P_2$ with $r = 1$, at which $y
= 1$. The orders of $\bar v = (1, y, y^2, y^3/x)$ are $(0,
-2, -4, -3)$ at $P_1$ and $(0, 0, 0, 1)$ at $P_2$, so the least powers of $x$
that remove the poles are $\tau = (0, 1, 2, 1)$. The discriminant of $(1,
y/x, y^2/x^2, y^3/x^2)$ is $x^{-8} \disc(\bar v)$, of degree $6 - 8 = -2$,
and $2 = (3 - 1) + (1 - 1)$ is the ramification at infinity; so this is a
local integral basis at infinity, $\bar v$ is normal at infinity, and
\[
  g = 0 + 1 + 2 + 1 - 4 + 1 = 1 .
\]
The curve of Example~\ref{ex:fail} is elliptic.
\end{example}

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}
Run \seealg{IntegralBasis} on $y^3 - x^2 (x - 1)$ and compare the result with
the basis~\eqref{eq:radical-basis}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that an integral basis $w$ has exactly one element in $K$, up to a
change of basis over $K$, when $K$ is algebraically closed in $E$. (Use
Lemma~\ref{lem:termwise}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Let $w$ be an integral basis normal at infinity. Show that the space of
$f \in E$ with no finite poles and poles of order at most $N r_P$ at each
infinite place $P$ has dimension $\sum_i \max(0, N - \tau_i + 1)$ over $K$,
and compare with Theorem~\ref{thm:riemann-roch}(iii).
\end{exercise}
