\chapter{The Hermite Reduction}\label{ch:hermite}

Hermite's reduction~\cite{hermite1872} writes a rational function as a
derivative plus a rational function whose denominator is squarefree, using
nothing more than gcd computations \VolI{\S2.2}. Volume~I carries it to a
monomial extension $k(t)$, where the remaining integrand is \emph{simple}: its
denominator has no multiple normal factor \VolI{\S5.3}. This chapter carries it
one step further, to a finite algebraic extension $E$ of $k(t)$. The result
has the same shape: any $f \in E$ can be written
\[
  f = Dg + h, \qquad g, h \in E,
\]
where $h$ has only simple poles at the normal places of $E$.

Two things are new. The first is that ``numerator'' and ``denominator'' need a
basis of $E$ to mean anything, and the reduction works for some bases and not
for others. Trager~\cite{trager1984} showed that it always works for an
integral basis. The second is Bronstein's observation~\cite{bronstein1998lazy}
that when the reduction fails for a given basis, the failure itself produces
an integral element outside the module the basis generates, so that the basis
can be improved exactly as far as the integrand requires and no further. We
prove the second statement and obtain the first as a corollary.

Following the report in which the lazy reduction first
appeared~\cite{bronstein1998lazy}, the chapter is written over a monomial
extension from the start. The reader interested only in algebraic functions
should take $k = K$ a field of constants, $t = x$ and $D = d/dx$ throughout; in
that case every squarefree polynomial is normal, there are no special
polynomials, and every $s$ and $d_s$ below is $1$. We call this the
\emph{pure algebraic case}. Chapter~\ref{ch:monomial} uses the general case.

\section{Numerators and denominators}\label{sec:hermite-numden}

Throughout this chapter, $(k, D)$ is a differential field of characteristic
$0$, $t$ is a monomial over $k$ \VolI{Definition~3.4.1}, and $E$ is a finite
algebraic extension of $k(t)$ of degree $n$. By \VolI{Theorem~3.2.3}, $D$
extends uniquely to a derivation of $E$, which we also write $D$. Recall that
$p \in k[t]$ is \emph{normal} if $\gcd(p, Dp) = 1$ and \emph{special} if
$p \mid Dp$ \VolI{Definition~3.4.2}; a normal polynomial is squarefree, and
factors of normal polynomials are normal \VolI{Theorem~3.4.1}.

We write $\OO$ for the integral closure of $k[t]$ in $E$
(Section~\ref{sec:prelim-integral}). It is a ring, and a free $k[t]$-module
of rank $n$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:closure-fg}). For a basis $w = (w_1, \dots, w_n)$ of $E$ over
$k(t)$ we write
\[
  \OO_w = k[t]\,w_1 + \dots + k[t]\,w_n
\]
for the $k[t]$-module it generates. We only ever use bases made of integral
elements, so that $\OO_w \subseteq \OO$; any basis can be made such by
multiplying each element by a suitable polynomial
(Proposition~\ref{prop:integral-basics}). The basis $w$ is an \emph{integral basis} if
$\OO_w = \OO$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:integral-bases}).

\begin{definition}\label{def:numden}
Let $w$ be a basis of $E$ over $k(t)$ and $f \in E$. Write $f = \sum_{i=1}^n
f_i w_i$ with $f_i \in k(t)$, and put the $f_i$ over their monic least common
denominator:
\[
  f = \frac{a_1 w_1 + \dots + a_n w_n}{d},
  \qquad a_1, \dots, a_n, d \in k[t], \quad \gcd(d, a_1, \dots, a_n) = 1.
\]
We call $a_1 w_1 + \dots + a_n w_n \in \OO_w$ the \emph{numerator} and $d$ the\index{denominator, with respect to a basis}
\emph{denominator of $f$ with respect to $w$}.
\end{definition}

Both are determined by $f$ and $w$, and $f \in \OO_w$ if and only if $d = 1$.
They do depend on $w$: enlarging $\OO_w$ can only shrink the denominator.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:den-divides}\standing{proved}{}
Let $w$ and $\bar w$ be bases of $E$ over $k(t)$ with $\OO_w \subseteq
\OO_{\bar w}$. Then for every $f \in E$ the denominator of $f$ with respect to
$\bar w$ divides its denominator with respect to $w$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $d$ be the denominator of $f$ with respect to $w$. Then $d f \in \OO_w
\subseteq \OO_{\bar w}$, so $d f = \sum_i c_i \bar w_i$ with $c_i \in k[t]$,
and the denominator of $f$ with respect to $\bar w$ is $d / \gcd(d, c_1,
\dots, c_n)$.
\end{proof}

Derivatives of integral elements need not be integral: for $y^2 = x$ we have
$dy/dx = y/(2x)$. So we record the denominators that differentiation
introduces.

\begin{definition}\label{def:suitable}
Let $w$ be a basis of $E$ over $k(t)$ with $w_i \in \OO$ for each $i$. The
\emph{derivation matrix} of $w$\index{derivation matrix} is the matrix $(m_{ij})$ over $k[t]$, together
with the monic $e_w \in k[t]$, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:derivation-matrix}
  D w_i = \frac{1}{e_w} \sum_{j=1}^n m_{ij} w_j \quad (1 \le i \le n),
  \qquad \gcd(e_w, m_{11}, m_{12}, \dots, m_{nn}) = 1.
\end{equation}
For each $i$ we write $e_i$ for the denominator of $D w_i$ with respect to $w$,
so that $e_w = \operatorname{lcm}(e_1, \dots, e_n)$. The basis $w$ is
\emph{suitable} if $e_w$ is normal\index{suitable basis}.
\end{definition}

The word is Bronstein's~\cite[\S3]{bronstein1998lazy}. In the pure algebraic
case, $w$ is suitable when $e_w$ is squarefree. We shall see
(Corollary~\ref{cor:integral-suitable}) that an integral basis is always
suitable, and (Example~\ref{ex:fail}) that a suitable basis need not be
integral.

Finally, the \emph{normal part}\index{normal part} of $p \in k[t] \setminus \{0\}$, written $p^*$,
is the product of the distinct monic normal irreducible factors of $p$. If
$p = p_s p_n$ is a splitting factorisation \VolI{Definition~3.5.1}, then $p^*$
is the squarefree part of $p_n$, so $p^*$ is computed by gcds alone. Up to a
unit, $p = p^*$ exactly when $p$ is normal.

\section{The derivative at a finite place}\label{sec:hermite-order}

In $k(t)$, differentiation lowers the order at a normal irreducible $p$ by
exactly one wherever that order is not zero, and does not lower the order at a
special one \VolI{Theorem~4.4.2}. Everything in this chapter rests on the
analogue for $E$, in which ``one'' becomes the ramification index.

We use the following from Chapter~\ref{ch:function-fields}
(Theorems~\ref{thm:places} and~\ref{thm:puiseux}). The field $E$ is
an algebraic function field of one variable over $k$. A \emph{finite place}
of $E$ is a place $P$ with $\ord{P}(t) \ge 0$; it lies over a unique monic
irreducible $p \in k[t]$, namely the one with $\ord{P}(p) > 0$, and
$\ord{P}(p)$ is the \emph{ramification index} of $P$. An element of $E$ is in
$\OO$ if and only if its order is nonnegative at every finite place. Finally,
Puiseux's theorem: if $P$ lies over $p$ with ramification index $r$, and
$\alpha \in \kbar$ is a root of $p$, then there is an embedding of fields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:puiseux}
  \sigma : E \longrightarrow \kbar((\pi)), \qquad
  \sigma(t) = \alpha + \pi^r, \qquad \sigma|_k = \mathrm{id},
\end{equation}
into the field of formal Laurent series in an indeterminate $\pi$, such that
$\ord{P}(f)$ is the order in $\pi$ of $\sigma(f)$ for every $f \in E$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:order-derivative}\standing{proved}{}
Let $P$ be a finite place of $E$ over the monic irreducible $p \in k[t]$, with
ramification index $r$, and let $f \in E \setminus \{0\}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item If $p$ is normal and $\ord{P}(f) \ne 0$, then $\ord{P}(Df) = \ord{P}(f) - r$.
\item If $p$ is normal and $\ord{P}(f) = 0$, then $\ord{P}(Df) \ge 1 - r$.
\item If $p$ is special, then $\ord{P}(Df) \ge \ord{P}(f)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Extend $D$ to $\kbar$, which can be done in exactly one way
\VolI{Theorem~3.2.3}, and take $\sigma$, $\alpha$ and $\pi$ as
in~\eqref{eq:puiseux}. Let $H \in k[t]$ be such that $Dt = H$, and put
\[
  \eta = \frac{H(\alpha + \pi^r) - D\alpha}{r\,\pi^{r-1}} \in \kbar((\pi)).
\]
Define $\Delta$ on $\kbar((\pi))$ by
\[
  \Delta \Bigl( \sum_{i \ge j} c_i \pi^i \Bigr)
    = \sum_{i \ge j} (Dc_i)\, \pi^i
      + \eta \sum_{i \ge j} i\, c_i\, \pi^{i-1} .
\]
It is the sum of the coefficientwise action of $D$ and of $\eta\, d/d\pi$, both
of which are derivations of $\kbar((\pi))$, so it is a derivation. It agrees
with $D$ on $k$, and
\[
  \Delta(\sigma(t)) = \Delta(\alpha + \pi^r) = D\alpha + r \pi^{r-1} \eta
    = H(\alpha + \pi^r) = \sigma(Dt),
\]
so $\Delta \circ \sigma$ and $\sigma \circ D$ agree on $k[t]$, hence on $k(t)$.
Both are derivations of $E$ into the field $\kbar((\pi))$, in which $E$ acts
through $\sigma$, and both extend the same derivation of $k(t)$; since $E$ is
separable algebraic over $k(t)$, they are equal. The argument is that of
\VolI{Theorem~3.2.3}: applying either one to the minimal polynomial equation of
an element of $E$ over $k(t)$ determines its value. Hence $\sigma(Df) = \Delta(\sigma(f))$ for all
$f \in E$, and it remains to read off orders in $\pi$.

The numerator of $\eta$ is the series $H(\alpha + \pi^r) - D\alpha$ in $\pi^r$,
with constant term $H(\alpha) - D\alpha$. If $p$ is normal, then $H(\alpha) \ne
D\alpha$ \VolI{Theorem~3.4.2}, so $\eta$ has order exactly $1 - r$. If $p$ is
special, then $H(\alpha) = D\alpha$ \VolI{Theorem~3.4.3}, so the numerator has
order at least $r$ and $\eta$ has order at least $1$.

Let $\sigma(f) = \sum_{i \ge j} c_i \pi^i$ with $c_j \ne 0$, so that $j =
\ord{P}(f)$. The first sum in $\Delta(\sigma(f))$ has order at least $j$. If
$j \ne 0$, the second has leading term $j c_j \pi^{j-1} \eta$, of order $j - r$
when $p$ is normal and at least $j$ when $p$ is special. If $j = 0$, the
second sum starts at $i = 1$ or later, so it has order at least $0 + (1 - r)$
when $p$ is normal and at least $1$ when $p$ is special. Since $r \ge 1$, the
three statements follow.
\end{proof}

For $k(t) = K(x)$ and $D = d/dx$, part~(i) is Lemma~4.2 of Trager's
thesis~\cite[\S4.2]{trager1984}, proved there the same way. Bronstein's
report takes the statement from Lemma~1.7 of~\cite{bronstein1990}.

The first consequence is the one the reduction turns on: a function whose
poles are not made worse by differentiation did not have those poles.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:lower-pole}\standing{proved}{}
Let $p \in k[t]$ be normal, $f \in E$ and $M \ge 1$ an integer. If $p^M f \in
\OO$ and $p^M Df \in \OO$, then $p^{M-1} f \in \OO$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose that $p^{M-1} f \notin \OO$. Then there is a finite place $P$ with
$\ord{P}(p^{M-1} f) < 0$. Since $\ord{P}(p^M f) \ge 0$, we have $\ord{P}(p) >
0$, so $P$ lies over an irreducible factor $p_1$ of $p$, which is normal, and
because $p$ is squarefree $\ord{P}(p) = \ord{P}(p_1) = r$, the ramification
index of $P$. Now $\ord{P}(f) < -(M-1) r \le 0$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative}(i) gives $\ord{P}(Df) = \ord{P}(f) - r$, and
\[
  \ord{P}(p^M Df) = M r + \ord{P}(f) - r = \ord{P}(p^{M-1} f) < 0,
\]
contradicting $p^M Df \in \OO$.
\end{proof}

The second consequence controls the denominators of derivatives.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:normal-part}\standing{proved}{}
Let $w \in \OO$ and $p \in k[t] \setminus \{0\}$. If $p\, Dw \in \OO$, then
$p^* Dw \in \OO$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $P$ be a finite place, over the irreducible $q$ with ramification index
$r$. If $q$ is special, then $\ord{P}(Dw) \ge \ord{P}(w) \ge 0$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative}(iii). If $q$ is normal and divides $p$, then
it divides $p^*$, and $\ord{P}(p^* Dw) \ge r + (-r) = 0$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative}(i) and~(ii). If $q$ is normal and does not
divide $p$, then $\ord{P}(Dw) = \ord{P}(p\, Dw) \ge 0$. In every case
$\ord{P}(p^* Dw) \ge 0$.
\end{proof}

\section{The reduction step}\label{sec:hermite-step}

Let $w$ be a basis of $E$ over $k(t)$ with $w_i \in \OO$, and let $f \in E$
have numerator $\sum_i a_i w_i$ and denominator $d$ with respect to $w$. Let $d
= d_s d_n$ be a splitting factorisation, and $d_n = d_1 d_2^2 \cdots d_m^m$ a
squarefree factorisation of $d_n$ with $\deg(d_m) > 0$. Suppose that $m \ge
2$, and let
\[
  s = d_s, \qquad v = d_m, \qquad u = d_n / v^m ,
\]
so that $d = s\,u\,v^m$, $v$ is normal, $s$ is special, and $s$, $u$ and $v$
are pairwise coprime. We look for $b_1, \dots, b_n \in k[t]$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hermite-goal}
  f = D\Bigl( \frac{b_1 w_1 + \dots + b_n w_n}{v^{m-1}} \Bigr) + h
\end{equation}
where the denominator of $h$ has no normal irreducible factor of multiplicity
$m$ or more. Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Si}
  S_i = s\,u\,v^m\, D\Bigl( \frac{w_i}{v^{m-1}} \Bigr)
      = s\,u\,v\, Dw_i - (m-1)\, s\,u\, (Dv)\, w_i
  \qquad (1 \le i \le n).
\end{equation}
Since $D(b_i w_i / v^{m-1}) = (Db_i) w_i / v^{m-1} + b_i S_i / (s u v^m)$, the
terms of~\eqref{eq:hermite-goal} with denominator $s u v^m$ cancel if $\sum_i
b_i S_i$ agrees with $\sum_i a_i w_i$ ``modulo $v$''. This leads to the linear
system
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hermite-system}
  f_1 S_1 + \dots + f_n S_n = a_1 w_1 + \dots + a_n w_n
\end{equation}
for $f_1, \dots, f_n \in k(t)$. Expressing each $S_i$ in the basis $w$
through~\eqref{eq:derivation-matrix} makes~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} an $n$ by
$n$ system over $k(t)$.

\begin{theorem}[the reduction step]\label{thm:hermite-step}\standing{proved}{}
With the notation above, suppose that~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} has a solution
$f_i = c_i / q$ with $q, c_1, \dots, c_n \in k[t]$ and $\gcd(q, v) = 1$. Let
$\rho, z \in k[t]$ be such that $\rho q + z v = 1$, and let $q_i, b_i \in
k[t]$ be the quotient and remainder of $\rho c_i$ by $v$:
\[
  \rho\, c_i = v\, q_i + b_i, \qquad b_i = 0 \text{ or } \deg(b_i) < \deg(v).
\]
Then~\eqref{eq:hermite-goal} holds with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hermite-h}
  h = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \bigl( z a_i - s u \,( (m-1) (Dv)\, q_i + D b_i ) \bigr) w_i}
           {s\,u\,v^{m-1}}
      + \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n q_i\, Dw_i}{v^{m-2}} .
\end{equation}
If in addition $w$ is suitable, then the denominator of $h$ with respect to
$w$ divides $s\,u\,v^{m-1}\,\tilde e$, where $\tilde e = e_w / \gcd(e_w, u v)$ is normal and
coprime with $s\,u\,v$. In particular its special factors are those of $s$, and it has no normal
irreducible factor of multiplicity $m$ or more.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $B = \sum_i b_i w_i$. By~\eqref{eq:Si},
\[
  f - D\Bigl(\frac{B}{v^{m-1}}\Bigr)
  = \frac{\sum_i a_i w_i - \sum_i b_i S_i}{s u v^m}
    - \frac{\sum_i (Db_i) w_i}{v^{m-1}} .
\]
Since $b_i = \rho c_i - v q_i$ and $\sum_i c_i S_i = q \sum_i a_i w_i$,
\[
  \sum_i a_i w_i - \sum_i b_i S_i
  = (1 - \rho q) \sum_i a_i w_i + v \sum_i q_i S_i
  = v \Bigl( z \sum_i a_i w_i + \sum_i q_i S_i \Bigr),
\]
and by~\eqref{eq:Si} again $S_i / (s u v^{m-1}) = Dw_i / v^{m-2} - (m-1) (Dv)
w_i / v^{m-1}$. Collecting terms gives~\eqref{eq:hermite-h}. Note that $Dv$
and the $Db_i$ are in $k[t]$, because $t$ is a monomial.

Suppose now that $w$ is suitable, and let $\gamma = \gcd(e_w, uv)$ and $\tilde e =
e_w / \gamma$. Since $e_w$ is normal, it is squarefree and coprime with $s$,
so $\tilde e$ is normal, coprime with $\gamma$ and with $s$; and any common factor of
$\tilde e$ and $uv$ would divide $\gamma$, so $\tilde e$ is coprime with $uv$.
By~\eqref{eq:derivation-matrix},
\[
  \frac{Dw_i}{v^{m-2}} = \frac{s u v\, Dw_i}{s u v^{m-1}}
  = \frac{s\,(uv/\gamma) \sum_j m_{ij} w_j}{\tilde e\, s u v^{m-1}} ,
\]
and $uv/\gamma \in k[t]$. Hence every term of~\eqref{eq:hermite-h} is an
element of $\OO_w$ divided by $\tilde e\,s\,u\,v^{m-1}$. Each irreducible factor of
$u$ has multiplicity at most $m - 1$ in $u$, $v$ is squarefree, and $\tilde e$ is
squarefree and coprime with $uv$, so no normal irreducible has multiplicity
$m$ or more in $\tilde e\,u\,v^{m-1}$.
\end{proof}

The theorem says nothing about when~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} has such a
solution, and it need not.

\begin{example}\label{ex:fail}
This is Example~1 of~\cite[\S2.1]{bronstein1998tutorial}. Let $K$ be a field
of characteristic $0$, $D = d/dx$,
\[
  E = K(x)[y] / (y^4 + (x^2 + x)\, y - x^2),
\]
and $w = (1, y, y^2, y^3)$, which consists of integral elements since the
defining polynomial is monic in $y$. Consider
\[
  f = \frac{y^3}{x^2} = \frac{w_4}{x^2} .
\]
Here $d = x^2$, so $s = u = 1$, $v = x$ and $m = 2$. Writing $F = 27 x^4 + 108
x^3 + 418 x^2 + 108 x + 27$, we find $S_1 = x^2 D(1/x) = -1$ and
\begin{align*}
  F\,S_2 &= 24 (1 - x^2)\, y^3 + 32 x (1 - x)\, y^2 \\
         &\qquad - (9 x^4 + 45 x^3 + 209 x^2 + 63 x + 18)\, y
           - 18 x (x - 1)(x + 1)^2 , \\
  F\,S_3 &= 64 x (1 - x)\, y^3 + 9 (x - 1)(x + 1)^3\, y^2 \\
         &\qquad + 12 x (x - 1)(x + 1)^2\, y + 48 x^2 (1 - x^2) , \\
  F\,S_4 &= x (27 x^3 + 81 x^2 + 209 x + 27)\, y^3 + 18 x (x - 1)(x + 1)^2\, y^2 \\
         &\qquad + 24 x^2 (x^2 - 1)\, y + 96 x^3 (1 - x) .
\end{align*}
The $S_i$ are linearly independent over $K(x)$, and the unique solution
of~\eqref{eq:hermite-system}, which here reads $\sum_i f_i S_i = y^3$, is
\[
  f_1 = f_2 = 0, \qquad f_3 = -2, \qquad f_4 = \frac{x + 1}{x} .
\]
Its denominator $q = x$ is not coprime with $v = x$, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:hermite-step} does not apply. Note that $e_w = F$ is
squarefree: the basis is suitable, and the reduction fails all the same.
\end{example}

\section{What a failure produces}\label{sec:hermite-failure}

The system~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} can fail to give a reduction step in two
ways: the $S_i$ can be linearly dependent over $k(t)$, or they can be
independent and the unique solution can have a denominator with a factor in
common with $v$. A third defect is that the basis may not be suitable, so that
the step, even when it succeeds, does not lower the multiplicity. In each
case the defect exhibits an element of $\OO$ that is not in $\OO_w$. The
three theorems of this section say which; they are Theorems~2 and~3 and
Section~3 of~\cite{bronstein1998lazy}, and Theorems~1, 2 and~3
of~\cite[\S2.1]{bronstein1998tutorial}, in that order.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:lazy-key}\standing{proved}{}
Let $w$ be a basis of $E$ over $k(t)$ with $w_i \in \OO$, let $A, B \in k[t]$
with $B$ normal, let $M \ge 1$ be an integer, and let
\[
  R_i = A\,B^{M+1}\, D\Bigl( \frac{w_i}{B^M} \Bigr) \qquad (1 \le i \le n).
\]
Let $c_1, \dots, c_n \in k[t]$ and let $G \in k[t]$ divide $B$. Then
\[
  \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^n c_i R_i \in \OO
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \frac{A\,(B/G)}{G} \sum_{i=1}^n c_i w_i \in \OO .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose first that $G = B$, and let $h = A \sum_i c_i w_i / B^M$. Then $B^M h
= A \sum_i c_i w_i \in \OO$, and
\[
  B^M Dh = B^M \sum_i A c_i\, D\Bigl(\frac{w_i}{B^M}\Bigr) + \sum_i D(A c_i)\, w_i
         = \frac{1}{B} \sum_i c_i R_i + \sum_i D(A c_i)\, w_i \in \OO .
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:lower-pole}, $B^{M-1} h = (A/B) \sum_i c_i w_i \in \OO$.

In general, let $H = B/G$. Differentiating $w_i / B^M = (w_i / G^M) H^{-M}$
gives
\[
  R_i = A\,H\,G^{M+1} D\Bigl(\frac{w_i}{G^M}\Bigr) - M A\, G\, (DH)\, w_i .
\]
Let $r_i = (AH)\, G^{M+1} D(w_i / G^M)$. Then
\[
  \frac{1}{G} \sum_i c_i r_i
  = \frac{1}{G} \sum_i c_i R_i + M A\, (DH) \sum_i c_i w_i \in \OO ,
\]
and $G$ is normal, being a factor of $B$. The first case, applied with $AH$
in place of $A$ and $G$ in place of $B$, gives $(AH/G) \sum_i c_i w_i \in
\OO$.
\end{proof}

In the next two theorems the notation is that of
Section~\ref{sec:hermite-step}: $d = s\,u\,v^m$ with $m \ge 2$, and the $S_i$
are given by~\eqref{eq:Si}. They are the $R_i$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:lazy-key}
for $A = s u$, $B = v$ and $M = m - 1$.

\begin{theorem}[dependent case]\label{thm:lazy-dependent}\standing{proved}{}
Suppose that $S_1, \dots, S_n$ are linearly dependent over $k(t)$, and let
$c_1, \dots, c_n \in k[t]$ be not all zero and such that $\sum_i c_i S_i = 0$.
Then
\[
  w_0 = \frac{s\,u}{v} \sum_{i=1}^n c_i w_i \in \OO .
\]
If moreover $\gcd(c_1, \dots, c_n) = 1$, then $w_0 \notin \OO_w$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $v^{-1} \sum_i c_i S_i = 0 \in \OO$, Lemma~\ref{lem:lazy-key} with $G =
v$ gives $w_0 \in \OO$. If $w_0 \in \OO_w$, then $v$ divides $s\,u\,c_i$ for
each $i$, because $w$ is a basis; as $v$ is coprime with $s u$, it divides
every $c_i$, which contradicts $\gcd(c_1, \dots, c_n) = 1$ since $\deg(v) >
0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[independent case]\label{thm:lazy-independent}\standing{proved}{}
Suppose that $S_1, \dots, S_n$ are linearly independent over $k(t)$, and let
$q, c_1, \dots, c_n \in k[t]$ be such that
\[
  \sum_{i=1}^n a_i w_i = \frac{1}{q} \sum_{i=1}^n c_i S_i .
\]
Let $\gamma = \gcd(v, q)$. Then
\[
  w_0 = \frac{s\,u\,(v/\gamma)}{\gamma} \sum_{i=1}^n c_i w_i \in \OO .
\]
If moreover $\gcd(q, c_1, \dots, c_n) = 1$ and $\deg(\gamma) > 0$, then $w_0
\notin \OO_w$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We have $\gamma^{-1} \sum_i c_i S_i = (q/\gamma) \sum_i a_i w_i \in \OO$, so
$w_0 \in \OO$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:lazy-key} with $G = \gamma$. If $w_0 \in
\OO_w$, then $\gamma$ divides $s\,u\,(v/\gamma)\,c_i$ for each $i$. Now
$\gamma$ is coprime with $s u$, and with $v/\gamma$ because $v$ is
squarefree, so $\gamma$ divides every $c_i$; it also divides $q$, which
contradicts $\gcd(q, c_1, \dots, c_n) = 1$ when $\deg(\gamma) > 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[unsuitable case]\label{thm:lazy-unsuitable}\standing{proved}{}
Let $w$ be a basis of $E$ over $k(t)$ with $w_i \in \OO$, and suppose that
the denominator $e_i$ of $Dw_i$ with respect to $w$ is not normal. Then
\[
  w_0 = e_i^*\, Dw_i \in \OO \setminus \OO_w .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $e_i\, Dw_i \in \OO_w \subseteq \OO$, Lemma~\ref{lem:normal-part} gives
$w_0 \in \OO$. The denominator of $w_0$ with respect to $w$ is $e_i / e_i^*$,
because the numerator of $Dw_i$ is coprime with $e_i$; and $e_i / e_i^*$ is
not a unit, because $e_i$ is not normal.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:fail-w0}
In Example~\ref{ex:fail} the $S_i$ are independent, with $q = x$ and $c =
(0, 0, -2x, x + 1)$. So $\gamma = \gcd(v, q) = x$, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:lazy-independent} asserts that
\[
  w_0 = \frac{1}{x} \bigl( -2x\, y^2 + (x + 1)\, y^3 \bigr)
      = -2 y^2 + (x + 1)\, \frac{y^3}{x}
\]
is integral over $K[x]$, although it is visibly not in $\OO_w$. Indeed $y^3/x$
is a root of
\[
  Z^4 + 3 (x + 1)\, Z^3 + 3 (x + 1)^2\, Z^2 + (x + 1)^3\, Z - x^2 ,
\]
which is monic with coefficients in $K[x]$.
\end{example}

\section{Trager's theorem}\label{sec:hermite-trager}

If $w$ is an integral basis, there is no element of $\OO$ outside $\OO_w$, so
none of the three defects can occur.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:integral-suitable}\standing{proved}{}
An integral basis is suitable. In particular, in the pure algebraic case, if
$w$ is an integral basis then the least common denominator $e_w$ of $Dw_1,
\dots, Dw_n$ is squarefree.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If $e_w$ is not normal then some $e_i$ is not normal, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:lazy-unsuitable} gives an element of $\OO \setminus \OO_w$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Trager]\label{thm:trager}\standing{proved}{}
Let $w$ be an integral basis, and let $f$, $d = s\,u\,v^m$ and $S_1, \dots,
S_n$ be as in Section~\ref{sec:hermite-step}, with $m \ge 2$. Then $S_1,
\dots, S_n$ are linearly independent over $k(t)$, and the unique solution
of~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} in $k(t)$ has a denominator coprime with $v$.
Consequently the reduction step of Theorem~\ref{thm:hermite-step} always
applies, and yields an $h$ whose denominator with respect to $w$ has no normal
irreducible factor of multiplicity $m$ or more.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If the $S_i$ were dependent, then clearing denominators and removing the
content of a nontrivial relation would give $c_i$ as in
Theorem~\ref{thm:lazy-dependent} with $\gcd(c_1, \dots, c_n) = 1$, hence an
element of $\OO \setminus \OO_w$. So they are independent, and the solution
of~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} is unique; write it $f_i = c_i / q$ with $\gcd(q,
c_1, \dots, c_n) = 1$. If $\gcd(v, q)$ had positive degree,
Theorem~\ref{thm:lazy-independent} would again give an element of $\OO
\setminus \OO_w$. The last statement is Theorem~\ref{thm:hermite-step},
since $w$ is suitable by Corollary~\ref{cor:integral-suitable}.
\end{proof}

For $k(t) = K(x)$ this is the theorem of~\cite[\S4.2]{trager1984}. Trager
states it as: the $S_i$ form a \emph{local integral basis} at $v$, that is,
every integral element is a combination of the $S_i$ with coefficients in
$K(x)$ whose denominators are coprime with $v$. His proof is direct, and is
in substance the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:lazy-key} run by contradiction; the
squarefreeness of $e_w$ is his Lemma~4.1.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:trager-form}
Trager arranges the computation differently.
Before reducing, he multiplies the numerator and the denominator of $f$ by
whatever is needed to make $e_w$ divide $u v$; the pair is then no longer in
lowest terms. With $u v = T e_w$, each $S_i$ lies in $\OO_w$, and instead of
solving~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} over the field one solves the congruences
\[
  a_i \equiv -(m - 1)\, s\,u\,(Dv)\, b_i + s\,T \sum_{j=1}^n b_j\, m_{ji}
  \pmod{v} \qquad (1 \le i \le n)
\]
for $b_1, \dots, b_n$ modulo $v$, which by Theorem~\ref{thm:trager} have a
unique solution. The system decouples, into $n$ congruences in one unknown
each, when $v$ is coprime with $e_w$, and when the derivation matrix is
diagonal. The second case is that of a simple radical extension, treated in
Chapter~\ref{ch:radical}. The Axiom implementation of the algebraic Hermite
reduction, which is Bronstein's, makes the same normalisation of the
denominator, and takes the derivation as an argument, so that it serves in
the mixed case as well.
\end{remark}

\begin{algo}{HermiteReduce}{f, w}{Hermite reduction over an integral basis}
\Spec{Given $f \in E$ and an integral basis $w$ of $E$ over $k(t)$, return
$g, h \in E$ such that $f = Dg + h$ and the denominator of $h$ with respect
to $w$ is $d_s d_n$ with $d_s$ special and $d_n$ normal.}
\State $g \gets 0$, \ $h \gets f$
\Loop
  \State $(a_1, \dots, a_n, d) \gets$ numerator and denominator of $h$ with respect to $w$
  \State $(d_s, d_n) \gets \alg{SplitFactor}(d, D)$ \Comment{Volume~I, \S3.5}
  \State $(d_1, \dots, d_m) \gets \alg{SquareFree}(d_n)$ \Comment{$\deg(d_m) > 0$, or $m = 1$}
  \If{$m \le 1$} \Return $(g, h)$ \EndIf
  \State $s \gets d_s$, \ $v \gets d_m$, \ $u \gets d_n / v^m$
  \For{$i \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $n$}
    \State $S_i \gets s\,u\,v\, Dw_i - (m - 1)\, s\,u\,(Dv)\, w_i$
  \EndFor
  \State $(c_1, \dots, c_n, q) \gets$ the solution $f_i = c_i / q$ of $\sum_i f_i S_i = \sum_i a_i w_i$
  \State $\rho \gets q^{-1} \bmod v$ \Comment{exists by Theorem~\ref{thm:trager}}
  \For{$i \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $n$}
    \State $b_i \gets \rho\, c_i \bmod v$
  \EndFor
  \State $B \gets (b_1 w_1 + \dots + b_n w_n) / v^{m-1}$
  \State $g \gets g + B$, \ $h \gets h - DB$
\EndLoop
\end{algo}

Each pass lowers by at least one the highest multiplicity $m$ of a normal
irreducible factor of the denominator of $h$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:trager}, so the loop is
executed fewer than $m$ times for the $m$ of the input. The coefficients of
$g$ in the basis $w$ are sums of fractions $b_i / v^{m-1}$ with $\deg(b_i) <
\deg(v)$; this is what Chapter~\ref{ch:algebraic-part} uses to deal with the
poles of $g$ at infinity.

\begin{example}\label{ex:running-hermite}
Let $E = \Q(x)[y]/(y^2 - (1 - x^3))$ and $D = d/dx$. Since $1 - x^3$ is
squarefree, $w = (1, y)$ is an integral basis (Theorem~\ref{thm:radical-basis}).
From $2 y\, Dy = -3x^2$ we get $Dy = 3x^2 y / (2 (x^3 - 1))$, so $e_w = x^3 -
1$, which is squarefree as Corollary~\ref{cor:integral-suitable} requires.

The two integrands that we follow through Chapters~\ref{ch:hermite}
to~\ref{ch:finite-order},
\[
  \frac{x}{\sqrt{1 - x^3}} = \frac{-x\, y}{x^3 - 1}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \frac{1}{x \sqrt{1 - x^3}} = \frac{-y}{x\,(x^3 - 1)},
\]
have squarefree denominators with respect to $w$, so \seealg{HermiteReduce}
returns them unchanged, with $g = 0$. To see the algorithm work, consider
instead
\[
  f = \frac{1}{(1 - x^3)^{3/2}} = \frac{y}{(x^3 - 1)^2} .
\]
\begin{enumerate}
\item $a = (0, 1)$ and $d = (x^3 - 1)^2$, so $s = u = 1$, $v = x^3 - 1$ and $m
  = 2$.
\item $S_1 = -Dv = -3x^2$, and $S_2 = v\, Dy - (Dv)\, y = \frac32 x^2 y - 3
  x^2 y = -\frac32 x^2 y$.
\item The system $f_1 S_1 + f_2 S_2 = y$ has the solution $f_1 = 0$, $f_2 =
  -2/(3x^2)$, so $(c_1, c_2) = (0, -2)$ and $q = 3x^2$, which is coprime
  with $v$.
\item Since $x \cdot x^2 = 1 + v$, we get $\rho = x/3$, and $b_2 = \rho\, c_2
  \bmod v = -2x/3$.
\item $B = -2 x y / (3 (x^3 - 1)) = 2x / (3y)$, and $h = f - DB = -y / (3
  (x^3 - 1)) = 1/(3y)$.
\end{enumerate}
The denominator of $h$ is squarefree and the algorithm stops:
\[
  \int \frac{dx}{(1 - x^3)^{3/2}}
  = \frac{2x}{3 \sqrt{1 - x^3}} + \frac13 \int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 - x^3}} .
\]
The integral that remains is not an elementary function
(Example~\ref{ex:first-kind}).
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:mixed-hermite}
The same algorithm in a mixed case. Let $k = \Q(x)$ with $D = d/dx$, let $t =
\log x$, so that $Dt = 1/x$, and let $E = k(t)[y]/(y^2 - t)$. The polynomial
$t$ is normal, since $\gcd(t, Dt) = 1$, and $w = (1, y)$ is an integral basis
because $t$ is squarefree. We have $Dy = y/(2xt)$, so $e_w = t$. Take
\[
  f = \frac{1}{x \log(x)^{3/2}} = \frac{(1/x)\, y}{t^2} .
\]
Then $s = u = 1$, $v = t$, $m = 2$, and $S_2 = t\, Dy - (Dt)\, y = -y/(2x)$.
The solution of~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} is $f_1 = 0$, $f_2 = -2$, so $q =
1$, $b_2 = -2$ and $B = -2y/t$. Then $h = f - DB = 0$, and
\[
  \int \frac{dx}{x \log(x)^{3/2}} = -\frac{2}{\sqrt{\log x}} .
\]
Had we taken $t = e^x$ instead, with $y^2 = t$, the polynomial $t$ would be
special, and an integrand such as $y / t^2$ would be returned unchanged: its
denominator $d = d_s = t^2$ has no normal factor. Poles at special places are
not the Hermite reduction's concern, here as in \VolI{\S5.3}.
\end{example}

\section{Extending a basis}\label{sec:hermite-extend}

Suppose that $w$ is a basis of $E$ over $k(t)$ with $w_i \in \OO$, and that
we have found $w_0 \in \OO \setminus \OO_w$. We want a basis of the module
$\OO_w + k[t]\, w_0$. Let $\sum_j c_j w_j$ and $c_0$ be the numerator and
denominator of $w_0$ with respect to $w$. Then $\OO_w + k[t]\, w_0$ is the set
of combinations, with coefficients in $k[t]$, of the rows of the $(n + 1)$ by
$n$ matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:extend-matrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    c_0   &     &        &     \\
        & c_0   &        &     \\
        &     & \ddots &     \\
        &     &        & c_0   \\
    c_1 & c_2 & \cdots & c_n
  \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
each row $(r_1, \dots, r_n)$ standing for the element $\sum_j r_j w_j / c_0$.
Since $k[t]$ is a Euclidean domain, row operations over $k[t]$ bring this
matrix to triangular form (\seealg{RowReduce},
Section~\ref{sec:prelim-hermite}), and since it
has rank $n$, the result has $n$ nonzero rows followed by a zero row.

\begin{algo}{ExtendBasis}{w, w_0}{adjoin an integral element to a basis}
\Spec{Given a basis $w$ of $E$ over $k(t)$ with $w_i \in \OO$, and $w_0 \in
\OO$, return a basis $\bar w$ of $E$ over $k(t)$ with $\bar w_i \in \OO$ and
$\OO_{\bar w} = \OO_w + k[t]\, w_0$.}
\State $(c_1, \dots, c_n, c_0) \gets$ numerator and denominator of $w_0$ with respect to $w$
\State $C \gets \alg{RowReduce}$ of the matrix~\eqref{eq:extend-matrix} \Comment{row $n + 1$ is zero}
\For{$i \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $n$}
  \State $\bar w_i \gets (C_{i1} w_1 + \dots + C_{in} w_n) / c_0$
\EndFor
\State \Return $(\bar w_1, \dots, \bar w_n)$
\end{algo}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:extend}\standing{proved}{}
\seealg{ExtendBasis} is correct. Moreover, any sequence of bases $w^{(0)},
w^{(1)}, \dots$ of $E$ over $k(t)$ with $\OO_{w^{(0)}} \subsetneq
\OO_{w^{(1)}} \subsetneq \dots \subseteq \OO$ is finite.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Row operations over $k[t]$ do not change the module generated by the rows, so
$\OO_{\bar w} = \OO_w + k[t]\, w_0$. This module contains $\OO_w$, so $\bar w$
spans $E$ over $k(t)$, and having $n$ elements it is a basis. It is contained
in $\OO$, since $\OO$ is a $k[t]$-module containing $w_0$ and the $w_i$. For
the second statement, $\OO$ is a finitely generated module over the Noetherian
ring $k[t]$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:closure-fg}), so every ascending chain of
submodules of $\OO$ is stationary.
\end{proof}

The chain can be bounded. The discriminant of a basis in $\OO$ is a nonzero
element of $k[t]$, and passing from $w$ to a basis $\bar w$ with $\OO_w
\subsetneq \OO_{\bar w}$ divides it by the square of a polynomial of positive
degree (Corollary~\ref{cor:disc-drops}). So there are at most
$\frac12 \deg(\disc(w))$ extensions, starting from $w$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:fail-extend}
In Example~\ref{ex:fail-w0}, the numerator and denominator of $w_0$ with
respect to $w = (1, y, y^2, y^3)$ are $(0, 0, -2x, x + 1)$ and $x$. Adding
twice the third row of
\[
  \begin{pmatrix}
    x &   &     &       \\
      & x &     &       \\
      &   & x   &       \\
      &   &     & x     \\
    0 & 0 & -2x & x + 1
  \end{pmatrix}
\]
to the last leaves $(0, 0, 0, x + 1)$ there, and the fourth and last rows
then combine to $(0, 0, 0, 1)$. The Hermite normal form has rows $(x, 0, 0,
0)$, $(0, x, 0, 0)$, $(0, 0, x, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 0, 1)$, so
\[
  \bar w = \Bigl( 1,\; y,\; y^2,\; \frac{y^3}{x} \Bigr) .
\]
With respect to $\bar w$ the integrand $y^3/x^2 = \bar w_4 / x$ of
Example~\ref{ex:fail} has the squarefree denominator $x$: the reduction is
finished, with $g = 0$, by a change of basis alone. The discriminant has
gone from $-x^4 F$ to $-x^2 F$. The reduction did not need to know
whether $\bar w$ is an integral basis. It is one
(Example~\ref{ex:ib-quartic}).
\end{example}

\section{The lazy Hermite reduction}\label{sec:hermite-lazy}

We can now reduce with respect to any basis of integral elements, repairing it
as we go.

\begin{algo}{MakeSuitable}{w}{enlarge a basis until it is suitable}
\Spec{Given a basis $w$ of $E$ over $k(t)$ with $w_i \in \OO$, return a
suitable basis $\bar w$ with $\bar w_i \in \OO$ and $\OO_w \subseteq
\OO_{\bar w}$.}
\Loop
  \State $(e_1, \dots, e_n) \gets$ the denominators of $Dw_1, \dots, Dw_n$ with respect to $w$
  \If{$e_i$ is normal for each $i$} \Return $w$ \EndIf
  \State $i \gets$ an index such that $e_i$ is not normal
  \State $w \gets \alg{ExtendBasis}(w,\, e_i^*\, Dw_i)$ \Comment{Theorem~\ref{thm:lazy-unsuitable}}
\EndLoop
\end{algo}

\begin{algo}{LazyHermiteReduce}{f, w}{lazy Hermite reduction}
\Spec{Given $f \in E$ and a basis $w$ of $E$ over $k(t)$ with $w_i \in \OO$,
return $g, h \in E$ and a suitable basis $\bar w$ with $\bar w_i \in \OO$ and
$\OO_w \subseteq \OO_{\bar w}$, such that $f = Dg + h$ and the denominator of
$h$ with respect to $\bar w$ is $d_s d_n$ with $d_s$ special and $d_n$
normal.}
\State $g \gets 0$, \ $h \gets f$, \ $w \gets \alg{MakeSuitable}(w)$
\Loop
  \State $(a_1, \dots, a_n, d) \gets$ numerator and denominator of $h$ with respect to $w$
  \State $(d_s, d_n) \gets \alg{SplitFactor}(d, D)$
  \State $(d_1, \dots, d_m) \gets \alg{SquareFree}(d_n)$ \Comment{$\deg(d_m) > 0$, or $m = 1$}
  \If{$m \le 1$} \Return $(g, h, w)$ \EndIf
  \State $s \gets d_s$, \ $v \gets d_m$, \ $u \gets d_n / v^m$
  \For{$i \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $n$}
    \State $S_i \gets s\,u\,v\, Dw_i - (m - 1)\, s\,u\,(Dv)\, w_i$
  \EndFor
  \If{$S_1, \dots, S_n$ are linearly dependent over $k(t)$}
    \State $(c_1, \dots, c_n) \gets$ coprime polynomials, not all $0$, with $\sum_i c_i S_i = 0$
    \State $w_0 \gets (s\,u / v) \sum_i c_i w_i$ \Comment{Theorem~\ref{thm:lazy-dependent}}
    \State $w \gets \alg{MakeSuitable}(\alg{ExtendBasis}(w, w_0))$
  \Else
    \State $(c_1, \dots, c_n, q) \gets$ the solution $f_i = c_i / q$ of $\sum_i f_i S_i = \sum_i a_i w_i$ \Comment{$\gcd(q, c_1, \dots, c_n) = 1$}
    \State $\gamma \gets \gcd(v, q)$
    \If{$\deg(\gamma) > 0$}
      \State $w_0 \gets (s\,u\,(v/\gamma) / \gamma) \sum_i c_i w_i$ \Comment{Theorem~\ref{thm:lazy-independent}}
      \State $w \gets \alg{MakeSuitable}(\alg{ExtendBasis}(w, w_0))$
    \Else
      \State $\rho \gets q^{-1} \bmod v$
      \For{$i \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $n$}
        \State $b_i \gets \rho\, c_i \bmod v$
      \EndFor
      \State $B \gets (b_1 w_1 + \dots + b_n w_n) / v^{m-1}$ \Comment{Theorem~\ref{thm:hermite-step}}
      \State $g \gets g + B$, \ $h \gets h - DB$
    \EndIf
  \EndIf
\EndLoop
\end{algo}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:lazy}\standing{proved}{}
\seealg{MakeSuitable} and \seealg{LazyHermiteReduce} terminate and are
correct.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each call of \seealg{ExtendBasis} in either algorithm is made with a $w_0 \in
\OO \setminus \OO_w$, by Theorems~\ref{thm:lazy-dependent},
\ref{thm:lazy-independent} and~\ref{thm:lazy-unsuitable}, and so replaces $w$
by a basis of integral elements generating a strictly larger module. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:extend} this happens finitely many times in all. That settles
\seealg{MakeSuitable}, whose loop does nothing else and which returns only a
basis all of whose $e_i$, hence whose $e_w$, are normal.

In \seealg{LazyHermiteReduce}, $f = Dg + h$ holds throughout, and $w$ is
suitable at the top of each pass. Let $m$ be the highest multiplicity of a normal irreducible factor
of the denominator of $h$ with respect to the current basis. A
pass that changes the basis does not increase $m$, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:den-divides}. A pass that does not change the basis performs a
reduction step, which by Theorem~\ref{thm:hermite-step} lowers $m$ by at
least one. Since there are finitely many passes of the first kind, the
algorithm reaches $m \le 1$ and returns; at that point $d_n$ is squarefree
with all its squarefree factors normal, so it is normal.
\end{proof}

When $w$ is an integral basis, no branch that changes the basis is ever
taken, and \seealg{LazyHermiteReduce} is \seealg{HermiteReduce}.

Bronstein's report estimates the gain: each step of the lazy reduction is an
elimination on a matrix with $n$ or $n + 1$ rows and $n$ columns, where
computing an integral basis requires eliminations on matrices with $n^2$ rows,
so that the lazy reduction ``is expected to cost'' $O(n^3)$ operations in
$k(t)$ against $O(n^4)$~\cite[Conclusions]{bronstein1998lazy}. The estimate is
his and is not an analysis. The basis returned need not be integral, and the
later stages of the integration algorithm, as this book presents them, do
require an integral basis (Chapters~\ref{ch:algebraic-part}
and~\ref{ch:logarithmic-part}); for those the lazy reduction postpones the
computation and does not remove it. What it does give on its own is the
reduction to simple poles, in any tower of monomial and algebraic extensions,
by rational operations.

\begin{example}\label{ex:lazy-full}
We run \seealg{LazyHermiteReduce} on
\[
  f = \frac{y^3}{x^3}, \qquad y^4 + (x^2 + x)\, y - x^2 = 0 ,
\]
from the basis $w = (1, y, y^2, y^3)$ of Example~\ref{ex:fail}, which is
suitable, with $e_w = F$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item With respect to $w$, $d = x^3$, so $s = u = 1$, $v = x$ and $m = 3$. The
  $S_i = x^3 D(w_i / x^2)$ are independent, and the solution
  of~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} has the denominator $q = 4x^3 + 49x^2 + 23x +
  10$, which is coprime with $x$. Reducing $\rho\, c_i$ modulo $x$ gives $(b_1,
  b_2, b_3, b_4) = (0, 0, 0, -1)$, so the step succeeds, with $B = -y^3/x^2$.
  The new integrand $h = f - DB$ is
  \begin{multline*}
    x^2 F\, h = -96 x^2 (x - 1) - 24 x (1 - x^2)\, y \\
              + 18 (x - 1)(x + 1)^2\, y^2
              + (27 x^3 + 81 x^2 + 209 x + 27)\, y^3 .
  \end{multline*}
\item Now $d = x^2 F$, so $v = x$, $m = 2$ and $u = F$. The $S_i = F x^2 D(w_i
  / x)$ are independent, and the solution of~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} is $f_1
  = f_2 = f_3 = 0$, $f_4 = 1/x$. So $q = x$ and $\gamma = \gcd(v, q) = x$: the
  step fails, and Theorem~\ref{thm:lazy-independent} gives $w_0 = F y^3 / x \in
  \OO \setminus \OO_w$. Since $F$ is coprime with $x$,
  \seealg{ExtendBasis} returns $\bar w = (1, y, y^2, y^3/x)$, the basis of
  Example~\ref{ex:fail-extend}. Its derivation matrix has $e_{\bar w} = x F$,
  which is squarefree, so $\bar w$ is suitable.
\item With respect to $\bar w$, the numerator of $h$ has coordinates
  \[
    \bigl( -96 x^2 (x - 1),\ -24 x (1 - x^2),\ 18 (x - 1)(x + 1)^2,\
           x\, (27 x^3 + 81 x^2 + 209 x + 27) \bigr)
  \]
  and its denominator is still $x^2 F$, so again $v = x$, $m = 2$, $u = F$.
  With the new $S_i = F x^2 D(\bar w_i / x)$, the solution
  of~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} is $(f_1, f_2, f_3, f_4) = (0, 0, 2, -x)$, so
  $q = 1$ and $(b_1, b_2, b_3, b_4) = (0, 0, 2, 0)$. The step succeeds, with
  $B = 2 \bar w_3 / x = 2 y^2 / x$.
\item The integrand is now
  \begin{multline*}
    x F\, h = 96 x^2 (x - 1)\, \bar w_1 - 24 x (x^2 - 1)\, \bar w_2 \\
              - 18 (x - 1)(x + 1)^2\, \bar w_3
              + (27 x^3 + 209 x^2 + 81 x + 27)\, \bar w_4 ,
  \end{multline*}
  so its denominator $x F$ is squarefree, and the algorithm returns.
\end{enumerate}
So
\[
  \int \frac{y^3}{x^3}\, dx = \frac{2 x y^2 - y^3}{x^2} + \int h\, dx .
\]
The first step succeeded over a basis that the second showed not to be
integral: a successful step proves nothing about the basis. The factor $F$ in
the final denominator is the $\tilde e$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:hermite-step}; it comes
from the basis, not from $f$.
\end{example}

\section{The polynomial reduction\later}\label{sec:hermite-polynomial}

The Hermite reduction lowers finite poles and stops when they are simple. It
does not decide whether what is left is a derivative. In this section we are
in the pure algebraic case, $E$ finite over $K(x)$ with $D = d/dx$, and we
say that $h \in E$ is \emph{integrable in $E$} if $h = DH$ for some $H \in
E$. The first step is easy.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:integrable-remainder}\standing{proved}{}
Let $w$ be an integral basis of $E$ over $K(x)$, and let $h \in E$ have a
squarefree denominator with respect to $w$. If $h = DH$ with $H \in E$, then
$H \in \OO$, and the denominator of $h$ with respect to $w$ divides $e_w$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $P$ be a finite place over the irreducible $p$, with ramification index
$r$. The denominator of $h$ is squarefree and the $w_i$ are integral, so
$\ord{P}(h) \ge -r$. If $\ord{P}(H) < 0$, then
Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative}(i) gives $\ord{P}(h) = \ord{P}(H) - r < -r$.
Hence $H \in \OO = \OO_w$. Write $H = \sum_i b_i w_i$ with $b_i \in K[x]$; then
$h = \sum_i (Db_i) w_i + \sum_i b_i\, Dw_i$ has a denominator dividing $e_w$.
\end{proof}

So after \seealg{HermiteReduce}, an $h$ whose denominator has a factor
outside $e_w$ is not integrable in $E$, and otherwise the question is whether
$h$ lies in the image of $\OO$ under $D$. That is a question of linear
algebra over $K$, but in a space of infinite dimension. Trager answers it
through the behaviour at infinity, which is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{ch:algebraic-part}. Chen, Kauers and Koutschan~\cite{chen2016}
answer it by a second reduction, which confines the remainder to a space of
finite dimension. We state their result; it needs the notion of a basis
\emph{normal at infinity}, for which see Chapter~\ref{ch:integral-bases}.

Let $\nu = (\nu_1, \dots, \nu_n)$ be a basis of $E$ over $K(x)$, and let $a
\in K[x]$ and the matrix $(\beta_{ij})$ over $K[x]$ be such that $a\, D\nu_i =
\sum_j \beta_{ij} \nu_j$. For a row vector $p = (p_1, \dots, p_n) \in K[x]^n$,
\[
  D\Bigl( \sum_i p_i \nu_i \Bigr) = \frac{1}{a} \sum_j \phi_\nu(p)_j\, \nu_j,
  \qquad \phi_\nu(p) = a\, Dp + p\, (\beta_{ij}) .
\]
The map $\phi_\nu : K[x]^n \to K[x]^n$ is $K$-linear. Let $N_\nu$ be the
$K$-subspace of $K[x]^n$ spanned by those monomials $x^j \epsilon_i$,
$\epsilon_i$ the $i$-th unit vector, that are not the leading term of any
element of the image of $\phi_\nu$. Then $K[x]^n = \operatorname{im}(\phi_\nu)
\oplus N_\nu$, and writing a vector as the sum of its two components is the
\emph{polynomial reduction} with respect\index{polynomial reduction} to
$\nu$~\cite[Definitions~9 and~10]{chen2016}.

\begin{theorem}[Chen, Kauers, Koutschan]\label{thm:additive}\standing{cited}{\cite[Lemma~12, Theorem~13]{chen2016}}
Let $w$ be an integral basis of $E$ over $K(x)$ that is normal at infinity.
Then there are integers $\tau_1, \dots, \tau_n \ge 0$ such that $\nu =
(x^{-\tau_1} w_1, \dots, x^{-\tau_n} w_n)$ is integral at infinity, and an
integer $\lambda \ge 0$ such that $a = x^\lambda e_w$ serves as the $a$ above
for $\nu$; and the space $N_\nu$ has finite dimension over $K$. Every $f \in
E$ can be written
\[
  f = Dg + \frac{1}{d} \sum_{i=1}^n p_i w_i
         + \frac{1}{x^\lambda e_w} \sum_{i=1}^n q_i \nu_i
\]
with $g \in E$, $d \in K[x]$ squarefree and coprime with $e_w$, $p_i \in K[x]$
with $\deg(p_i) < \deg(d)$, and $(q_1, \dots, q_n) \in N_\nu$. Moreover $f$ is
integrable in $E$ if and only if all the $p_i$ and all the $q_i$ are zero.
\end{theorem}

The decomposition is computed by \seealg{HermiteReduce}, a partial fraction
split of the remainder into the part with denominator $d$ and the part with
denominator $e_w$, and the polynomial reduction of the second part. It is
called an \emph{additive decomposition} of $f$\index{additive decomposition}. Its authors' purpose was
creative telescoping, where the finite dimension of the space of remainders
bounds the order of a telescoper; for integration, it gives a test for
integrability in $E$ that does not change the variable to move a point to
infinity.

Chen, Du and Kauers~\cite{chen2021} carry this over to the lazy reduction.
Proposition~\ref{prop:integrable-remainder} has an analogue for suitable
bases, and the polynomial reduction is performed with respect to a basis
that is \emph{suitable at infinity}, a condition on the degrees of its
derivation matrix that plays the part of normality at infinity. The outcome
is an additive decomposition with respect to a suitable basis, without an
integral basis being computed~\cite[Theorem~16]{chen2021}. We have not
reproduced the details.

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}
Let $E = K(x)[y]/(y^2 - x)$ and $w = (1, y)$. Compute $e_w$, and reduce $f =
y/x^2$ with \seealg{HermiteReduce}. What does the algorithm do with $f =
y/x$, and what is its integral?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Let $E = K(x)[y]/(y^2 - x^3)$ and $w = (1, y)$. Show that $w$ is suitable and
is not an integral basis, and that nevertheless the reduction step of
Theorem~\ref{thm:hermite-step} succeeds with respect to $w$ for every
integrand. So \seealg{LazyHermiteReduce} need never discover that a basis is
not integral.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that in Theorem~\ref{thm:hermite-step} the $b_i$ do not depend on the
choice of $\rho$. Show that when $w$ is an integral basis they are the only
polynomials of degree less than $\deg(v)$ for which~\eqref{eq:hermite-goal}
holds with an $h$ whose denominator divides $s\,u\,v^{m-1}\,\tilde e$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Let $P$ be a finite place over a normal irreducible $p$, with ramification
index $r$. Use Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative} to show that for $f \in E
\setminus \{0\}$ the order of $Df/f$ at $P$ is at least $-r$, with equality if
and only if $\ord{P}(f) \ne 0$. This is the analogue of
\VolI{Corollary~4.4.2}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Verify the claim of Example~\ref{ex:lazy-full} that $e_{\bar w} = x F$, and
find the solution of~\eqref{eq:hermite-system} for $f = y^2/x^2$ with respect
to $\bar w$. Deduce that
\[
  \int \frac{y^2}{x^2}\, dx = -\frac{3 y^2}{x} + \int h\, dx
\]
for an $h$ whose denominator with respect to $\bar w$ is squarefree.
\end{exercise}
