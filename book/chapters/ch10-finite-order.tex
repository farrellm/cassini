\chapter{Points of Finite Order}\label{ch:finite-order}

One thing is missing from the algorithm of
Chapter~\ref{ch:logarithmic-part}. Given a divisor $\delta$ of degree $0$,
we can test whether $\delta$ is principal, and $2\delta$, and $3\delta$; if
some multiple is principal the search finds it. If none is, the search does
not end. This chapter supplies the bound that ends it.

The problem does not arise for rational functions, nor on any curve of genus
$0$, where every divisor of degree $0$ is principal
(Corollary~\ref{cor:rr-consequences}). On a curve of positive genus the
classes of divisors of degree $0$ modulo principal divisors form a group that
is not trivial, the group of points of the Jacobian variety of the curve, and
the question is whether the class of $\delta$ has finite order in it: whether
it is a \emph{torsion} class\index{torsion}, or, on an elliptic curve, a \emph{point of
finite order}. This is the question that Hardy thought had no general
answer (Section~\ref{sec:liouville-history}).

The answer, due to Risch~\cite{risch1970} and made into an algorithm by
Trager~\cite[Chapter~6]{trager1984}, comes from reducing the curve modulo a
prime. Over a finite field the group of classes is finite, so every class has
finite order, bounded in terms of the genus and the size of the field; and
reduction modulo a suitable prime does not shrink the order of a torsion
class, apart from a power of the characteristic. Two primes dispose of the
exception.

This chapter rests on theorems of arithmetic geometry that we cite and do
not prove. What it adds is the precise form in which they are used, and the
arithmetic that turns them into a bound.

\section{Function fields over finite fields}\label{sec:fo-finite}

Let $\tilde E$ be an algebraic function field of genus $g$ whose exact
constant field is the finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$. Let
$\operatorname{Cl}^0(\tilde E)$ be the group of divisors of degree $0$ of
$\tilde E$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$ modulo principal divisors.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:class-number}\standing{cited}{\cite[Proposition~5.1.3, Theorems~5.1.15 and~5.2.1]{stichtenoth2009}; \cite[part~II, \S IV]{weil1948}}
$\operatorname{Cl}^0(\tilde E)$ is a finite group. Its order $h$, the
\emph{class number} of $\tilde E$\index{class number}, is $L(1)$, where $L(u) = \prod_{i=1}^{2g}
(1 - \alpha_i u)$ is the numerator of the zeta function of $\tilde E$, and
$|\alpha_i| = \sqrt q$ for each $i$. Consequently
\[
  (\sqrt q - 1)^{2g} \le h \le (\sqrt q + 1)^{2g} .
\]
\end{theorem}

The statement about the $\alpha_i$ is the Riemann hypothesis for curves over
finite fields, proved by Weil; the bounds on $h$ are read off $h = \prod_i (1
- \alpha_i)$. For $g = 1$ and $\tilde E$ the function field of an elliptic
curve, $h$ is the number of points of the curve over $\mathbb{F}_q$,
including the point at infinity.

So over a finite field every divisor of degree $0$ has a principal multiple
$n \tilde\delta$ with $n$ dividing $h$, and \seealg{PrincipalMultiple} with
$B = h$, or with the bound $(\sqrt q + 1)^{2g}$ if $h$ is not known, finds the
exact order of the class. The algorithms of Chapters~\ref{ch:integral-bases}
and~\ref{ch:divisors} work over a finite field whose characteristic exceeds
the degree of $\tilde E$ over $\mathbb{F}_q(x)$, the restriction coming from the trace radical of
Theorem~\ref{thm:trager-criterion}.

\section{Good reduction}\label{sec:fo-reduction}

Let $K$ be a number field, $\mathfrak p$ a prime ideal of its ring of
integers, $\mathbb{F}_q$ the residue field and $p$ its characteristic. Let
$E = K(x)[y]/(F)$ with $F$ monic in $y$, absolutely irreducible, and with
coefficients integral at $\mathfrak p$; reducing the coefficients gives
$\tilde F \in \mathbb{F}_q[x][y]$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:good-reduction}
$E$ has \emph{good reduction}\index{good reduction} at $\mathfrak p$, with respect to $F$, if
$\tilde F$ is absolutely irreducible and the function field $\tilde E =
\mathbb{F}_q(x)[y]/(\tilde F)$ has the same genus as $E$.
\end{definition}

This is Trager's definition~\cite[\S6.2]{trager1984}. For a given $F$ it
holds at all but finitely many primes; the general statement, for a function
field over any field with a discrete valuation, is
Deuring's~\cite{deuring1941}. Trager's test for it uses nothing new: reduce
an integral basis of $E$ that is normal at infinity, and check that it is
still an integral basis of $\tilde E$ and that only one of its elements has
no pole at infinity. The first keeps the genus, by
Corollary~\ref{cor:genus-tau}, and the second is absolute irreducibility
(Section~\ref{sec:const-algorithm}).

Under good reduction, divisors of $E$ defined over $K$ reduce to divisors of
$\tilde E$, principal divisors to principal divisors, and so classes to
classes. The theorem that makes this useful is the following.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:reduction-torsion}\standing{cited}{\cite[Theorem~6.4]{trager1984}; \cite[Appendix]{katz1981}}
Let $E$ have good reduction at $\mathfrak p$, and let $\rho$ be the reduction
homomorphism from the classes of divisors of degree $0$ of $E$ that are
defined over $K$ to $\operatorname{Cl}^0(\tilde E)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $\rho$ is injective on the classes of finite order prime to $p$.
\item If the ramification index $e_{\mathfrak p}$ of $\mathfrak p$ over $p$
  satisfies $e_{\mathfrak p} < p - 1$, then $\rho$ is injective on all classes
  of finite order.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Trager states~(i) and attributes it to Risch and to Dwork and Baldassarri;
for the reduction of the Jacobian he cites Serre and
Tate~\cite{serretate1968} and Shimura and Taniyama~\cite{shimura1961}. Part~(ii)
is the lemma in the appendix of Katz's paper, stated there for the torsion
points of an abelian variety over a number field and proved through the
formal group; Katz calls it well known. In both, the group is the group of
points of the Jacobian, and the passage between divisor classes of the
function field and points of the Jacobian is part of what we take from the
sources. For $K = \Q$ we have $e_{\mathfrak p} = 1$, so~(ii) applies to every
prime $p \ge 3$ of good reduction.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:order-reduction}\standing{proved}{}
Let $E$ have good reduction at $\mathfrak p$, and let $c$ be a class of
finite order $p^j n$ with $\gcd(n, p) = 1$. Then $\rho(c)$ has order $p^i n$
for some $i \le j$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The order of $\rho(c)$ divides $p^j n$; write it $p^i m$ with $m \mid n$ and
$i \le j$. The class $p^j c$ has order exactly $n$, prime to $p$, so by
Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction-torsion}(i) its image $p^j \rho(c)$ has order
exactly $n$. But the order of $p^j \rho(c)$ divides $m$. Hence $m = n$.
\end{proof}

This is Corollary~6.5 of~\cite{trager1984}.

\section{The order from two primes}\label{sec:fo-two-primes}

One reduction determines the order of a torsion class up to a power of $p$.
A second, in a different characteristic, determines that power.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:two-primes}\standing{proved}{}
Let $E$ have good reduction at $\mathfrak p$ and at $\mathfrak q$, with
residue characteristics $p \ne q$, and reduction maps $\rho_{\mathfrak p}$,
$\rho_{\mathfrak q}$. Let $c$ be a class of degree $0$ defined over $K$, and
let $o_{\mathfrak p}$ and $o_{\mathfrak q}$ be the orders of $\rho_{\mathfrak
p}(c)$ and $\rho_{\mathfrak q}(c)$. Write $o_{\mathfrak p} = p^a\,
o_{\mathfrak p}'$ and $o_{\mathfrak q} = q^b\, o_{\mathfrak q}'$ with
$o_{\mathfrak p}'$ prime to $p$ and $o_{\mathfrak q}'$ prime to $q$, and let
$p^{j}$ be the exact power of $p$ dividing $o_{\mathfrak q}'$. If $c$ has
finite order, then that order is
\[
  N = p^{j}\, o_{\mathfrak p}' ,
\]
and the parts of $o_{\mathfrak p}'$ and of $o_{\mathfrak q}'$ prime to $p q$
are equal. In particular the order of a torsion class defined over $K$
divides $h_{\mathfrak p}'\, h_{\mathfrak q}^{(p)}$, where $h_{\mathfrak p}'$
is the part of the class number of the reduction at $\mathfrak p$ prime to
$p$, and $h_{\mathfrak q}^{(p)}$ is the power of $p$ in the class number of
the reduction at $\mathfrak q$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let the order of $c$ be $p^j q^k n$ with $n$ prime to $p q$. By
Corollary~\ref{cor:order-reduction} at $\mathfrak p$, $o_{\mathfrak p} = p^i
q^k n$ with $i \le j$, so $o_{\mathfrak p}' = q^k n$. At $\mathfrak q$,
$o_{\mathfrak q} = q^{i'} p^j n$, so $o_{\mathfrak q}' = p^j n$, and the power
of $p$ in it is $p^j$. The rest follows.
\end{proof}

This is the argument at the end of~\cite[\S6.2]{trager1984}, and the bound
in the last sentence is Risch's ``class numbers of two good
reductions''~\cite{risch1970}. It turns \seealg{PrincipalMultiple} into a
procedure with one test in characteristic $0$.

\begin{algo}{FiniteOrder}{\delta}{the order of a divisor class, by two reductions}
\Spec{Given a finite divisor $\delta$ of degree $0$ of $E$, defined over the
number field $K$, return $(N, u)$ with $N \ge 1$ least such that $N\delta$ is
principal and $N\delta = (u)$; or ``infinite'' if no multiple of $\delta$ is
principal.}
\State choose primes $\mathfrak p$, $\mathfrak q$ of $K$ of different residue characteristics $p$, $q$ at which $E$ and $\delta$ have good reduction
\State $o_{\mathfrak p} \gets$ the order of the class of $\tilde\delta$ in the reduction at $\mathfrak p$ \Comment{\seealg{PrincipalMultiple}, with the bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:class-number}}
\State $o_{\mathfrak q} \gets$ the same at $\mathfrak q$
\State $o_{\mathfrak p}' \gets$ the part of $o_{\mathfrak p}$ prime to $p$, \ $o_{\mathfrak q}' \gets$ the part of $o_{\mathfrak q}$ prime to $q$
\If{the parts of $o_{\mathfrak p}'$ and $o_{\mathfrak q}'$ prime to $pq$ differ} \Return ``infinite'' \EndIf
\State $N \gets o_{\mathfrak p}' \cdot (\text{the power of } p \text{ in } o_{\mathfrak q}')$
\State $u \gets \alg{PrincipalGenerator}(I(N\delta))$
\If{$u$ is ``not principal''} \Return ``infinite'' \EndIf
\State \Return $(N, u)$
\end{algo}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:finite-order}\standing{proved}{}
\seealg{FiniteOrder} is correct.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If the class of $\delta$ has finite order, Theorem~\ref{thm:two-primes} says
that the two parts agree and that the order is $N$, so $N\delta$ is principal
and the algorithm returns $(N, u)$; and $N$ is then least. If the class has
infinite order, no multiple is principal, in particular not $N\delta$, and
the algorithm returns ``infinite'' at one of its two exits.
\end{proof}

That the divisor has good reduction means that the pair $(h, a)$
representing it (Section~\ref{sec:div-ideals}) reduces to a representation of
the reduced divisor. Trager's conditions are that the leading coefficient of
$a$ does not vanish modulo the prime, so that no point of the support moves
to infinity, and that $a$ stays coprime with the polynomial whose roots are
the projections of the other zeros and poles of
$h$~\cite[\S6.3]{trager1984}. Almost all primes satisfy them.

With this, \seealg{LogarithmicPart} is an algorithm, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:integrate-algebraic} holds as stated, for $K$ a number
field. The work in characteristic $0$,
where arithmetic is expensive, is one principality test for each $\delta_j$;
the search is carried out over finite fields. Trager stresses this as the
practical point of the method.

\section{Examples}\label{sec:fo-examples}

\begin{example}\label{ex:fo-running}
For $y^2 = 1 - x^3$ over $\Q$, the discriminant of $1 - x^3$ is $-27$, and the
curve has good reduction at $5$ and at $7$, where the reduced cubic is
squarefree. Counting the solutions of $y^2 = 1 - x^3$ and adding the point at
infinity:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
$p$ & values of $1 - x^3$ for $x = 0, \dots, p - 1$ & points \\
\midrule
$5$ & $1, 0, 3, 4, 2$ & $2 + 1 + 0 + 2 + 0 + 1 = 6$ \\
$7$ & $1, 0, 0, 2, 0, 2, 2$ & $2 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 12$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
So $h_5 = 6$ and $h_7 = 12$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction-torsion}(ii), a
torsion class defined over $\Q$ has order dividing both, hence dividing $6$.
The search of Example~\ref{ex:principal-running} could have been cut off at
$B = 6$; in fact it stopped at $3$.

\seealg{FiniteOrder} on $\delta = P - Q$, $P = (0, 1)$, $Q = (0, -1)$: modulo
$5$ and modulo $7$ the points stay distinct and finite, and the computation
of Example~\ref{ex:principal-running} goes through unchanged, since it
involved no denominator but $2$. So $o_5 = o_7 = 3$, the parts
prime to $35$ agree, $N = 3$, and $3\delta$ is principal over $\Q$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:fo-infinite}
Consider
\[
  \int \frac{dx}{(x - 3) \sqrt{x^3 - 2}} ,
\]
in $E = \Q(x)[y]/(y^2 - x^3 + 2)$, of genus $1$. The differential $h\,dx$,
$h = 1/((x - 3)\,y)$, has no pole at infinity, where its order is $2 + 3 - 3 =
2$, and none at the three branch places. Its poles are at $P = (3, 5)$ and
$P' = (3, -5)$, with residues $\frac15$ and $-\frac15$, since $3^3 - 2 = 25$.
So $q_1 = \frac15$, $m = 1$ and $\delta = P - P'$.

The discriminant of $x^3 - 2$ is $-108 = -2^2 3^3$, so there is good reduction
at $5$ and at $7$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
$p$ & values of $x^3 - 2$ for $x = 0, \dots, p - 1$ & points \\
\midrule
$5$ & $3, 4, 1, 0, 2$ & $0 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 0 + 1 = 6$ \\
$7$ & $5, 6, 6, 4, 6, 4, 4$ & $0 + 0 + 0 + 2 + 0 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 7$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
So $h_5 = 6$ and $h_7 = 7$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction-torsion}(ii), the
order of a torsion class defined over $\Q$ divides $\gcd(6, 7) = 1$: the only
torsion class is the trivial one. But $\delta$ is not principal, because $P
\ne P'$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:rr-consequences}(ii)). So the class of $\delta$
has infinite order, no multiple of $\delta$ is principal, and by
Theorem~\ref{thm:log-part}(ii)
\[
  \int \frac{dx}{(x - 3) \sqrt{x^3 - 2}}
\]
is not an elementary function. Compare Example~\ref{ex:running-log}: the two
integrands have the same shape, and what separates them is the arithmetic of
one point on each curve.

\seealg{FiniteOrder} reaches the same verdict with less insight. Modulo $5$
the points $P$ and $P'$ both reduce to $(3, 0)$, so $\tilde\delta = 0$ and
$o_5 = 1$. Modulo $7$, $\tilde P = (3, 5)$ is a nonzero point in a group of
order $7$, so $o_7 = 7$. The parts prime to $35$ are both $1$, and $N = 1
\cdot 1 = 1$; the test of $\delta$ itself for principality fails, and the
answer is ``infinite''. A prime such as $5$, at which the support of the
divisor collapses, is excluded by Trager's conditions on the reduction of the
divisor; it does no harm here, but the conditions are what guarantee that
the reduced ideal represents the reduced divisor.
\end{example}

\section{Parameters and elliptic curves}\label{sec:fo-parameters}

Everything above assumed that $K$ is a number field. In general the field of
constants is finitely generated over $\Q$: a number field with some
transcendental parameters adjoined.

\paragraph{Specialisation.} Trager treats a parameter by one more reduction:
replace it by an integer. A field such as $\Q(s)$ has a discrete valuation
for each value $s \mapsto s_0$, good reduction is defined as before, and it
holds for all but finitely many $s_0$. The residue field has characteristic
$0$, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction-torsion}(i), read for such a valuation,
torsion classes keep their exact order. With $r$ parameters this gives $r$
specialisations followed by the two reductions modulo
primes~\cite[\S6.3]{trager1984}; Deuring's paper~\cite{deuring1941} covers
both kinds of reduction in the same terms. As with primes, a class of
infinite order can become torsion after specialisation, which is why the
candidate order must be tested in the original field.

\paragraph{The question for all values of the parameter.} A different
question is for which values of the parameter the integral becomes
elementary. Davenport devoted a chapter to it~\cite[Chapter~6]{davenport1981}
and conjectured, in effect, that if the integral is not elementary for the
generic value then it is elementary for at most finitely many special
values. Masser and Zannier~\cite{masser2020} show that the conjecture is
false as stated and prove a modified version, determining the
counterexamples, which they describe as rather rare; their example is that
there are at most finitely many complex $t$ for which
\[
  \int \frac{dx}{(x^2 - 1) \sqrt{x^5 + t x^3 + x}}
\]
is elementary. Zannier's survey~\cite{zannier2014} says of Davenport's
methods that they ``sometimes indeed may work'' but that ``a complete
treatment is not clarified''. This is arithmetic geometry and not an
algorithm; we mark it as the boundary of what the present method decides. For
a \emph{given} value of the parameter, \seealg{FiniteOrder} applies.

\paragraph{Elliptic curves over $\Q$.} For genus $1$ over the rationals,
Davenport's algorithm does not reduce modulo primes at all. It uses a theorem
of Mazur, which he quotes: on an elliptic curve defined over $\Q$, the
maximum order of an element of the torsion of the divisor class group is
$12$. So \seealg{PrincipalMultiple} with $B = 12$ is a complete algorithm in
that case, and Davenport observes that this ``resolves this case of the
problem posed by Hardy''~\cite[Chapter~5]{davenport1981}. His procedure, in
his Chapter~7, tests the multiples $2, \dots, 10$ and $12$, and over a number
field other than $\Q$ brings the curve to Weierstrass form and uses the
criterion of Lutz and Nagell. Example~\ref{ex:fo-running} is consistent with this: the bound found
there was $6$.

\begin{exercise}
Verify the two tables of this chapter, and compute $h_{11}$ for both curves.
What do the three class numbers together say about the torsion over $\Q$ of
$y^2 = 1 - x^3$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Use the method of Example~\ref{ex:fo-infinite} to decide whether
\[
  \int \frac{dx}{(x - 2) \sqrt{x^3 + 1}}
\]
is elementary. (The point $(2, 3)$ has finite order on $y^2 = x^3 + 1$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Let $y^2 = p(x)$ with $p \in \Z[x]$ squarefree of degree $5$. Show that a
torsion class over $\Q$ has order at most $(\sqrt 3 + 1)^4 < 56$ if the curve
has good reduction at $3$, and explain why the bound of
Theorem~\ref{thm:class-number} alone, without the class number itself,
already makes the search finite.
\end{exercise}
