\chapter{The Problem and Liouville's Theorem}\label{ch:liouville}

An \emph{algebraic function} is an element of a finite algebraic extension
$E$ of a rational function field $K(x)$. This part of the book is about one
question: given $f \in E$, is $\int f\,dx$ an elementary function, and if so,
which? This chapter states the question precisely, recalls what Liouville's
theorem says about the answer, sharpens it in the way the algorithm needs,
and outlines the algorithm that Chapters~\ref{ch:hermite}
to~\ref{ch:finite-order} develop.

\section{The classical problem}\label{sec:liouville-history}

For rational functions the question has had an answer since the eighteenth
century: the integral is always elementary, and partial fractions find it
\VolI{Chapter~2}. For algebraic functions it has no such answer. The integrals
$\int dx/\sqrt{1 - x^2}$ and $\int dx/\sqrt{1 - x^3}$ look alike, and the
first is an inverse sine while the second is not an elementary function at
all.

The theory begins with Liouville, who published eleven papers on integration
in finite terms between 1833 and 1841~\cite[Chapter~IX]{lutzen1990}. The
theorem that bears his name, in the form he gave it for algebraic integrands,
says that if $\int y\,dx$ is expressible in finite terms for an algebraic
function $y$ of $x$, then
\[
  \int y\,dx = t + A \log u + B \log v + \dots + C \log w
\]
with $A, B, \dots, C$ constants and $t, u, v, \dots, w$ algebraic functions of
$x$~\cite[p.~352]{lutzen1990}. The integral, if elementary, is elementary in a
very restricted way: no exponentials, no nested logarithms, and logarithms
entering only linearly with constant coefficients. Liouville used this to
prove particular integrals non-elementary, certain elliptic integrals among
them. L\"utzen's chapter is the history, including what Abel had done before
and what Liouville's successors did after.

A theorem about the form of the answer is not a method for finding it. Three
quarters of a century later Hardy, surveying the subject, wrote of elliptic
integrals that ``no method has been devised as yet by which we can always
determine in a finite number of steps whether a given elliptic integral is
pseudo-elliptic, and integrate it if it is, and there is reason to suppose that
no such method can be given''~\cite[pp.~47--48]{hardy1916}. (An elliptic
integral is \emph{pseudo-elliptic}\index{pseudo-elliptic integral} when it is elementary after all.) The
difficulty he had in mind is the one that Chapter~\ref{ch:finite-order} is
about. A candidate logarithm $\log u$ is determined by the places where $u$
must vanish or have poles, with their multiplicities, and there may be no
such $u$ while there is one with all the multiplicities doubled, or tripled,
or multiplied by any larger integer. One can try multiples in turn; the
question is when to stop.

Hardy's supposition was wrong. Risch announced a solution in 1970, in which
the problem ``reduces to the problem of bounding torsion on the Jacobian
variety of an algebraic curve''~\cite{risch1970}, and obtained a bound from two good
reductions of the curve in different characteristics. Davenport~\cite{davenport1981} and
Trager~\cite{trager1984} then gave algorithms meant to be programmed, the
first by Puiseux expansions and the second by integral bases and ideals.
Trager's is the one this book follows.

\section{Function fields and their differentials}\label{sec:liouville-setting}

Throughout Part~II, $K$ is a field of characteristic $0$, $x$ is transcendental
over $K$, and
\[
  E = K(x)[y] / (F)
\]
where $F \in K[x][y]$ is monic of degree $n$ in $y$ and absolutely
irreducible, that is, irreducible over $\Kbar(x)$. Chapter~\ref{ch:constant-field} explains why
this assumption costs nothing and how to arrange it. Its consequences are
that $K$ is algebraically closed in $E$, and that for every algebraic
extension $L$ of $K$,
\[
  E_L = L(x)[y] / (F)
\]
is a field, of degree $n$ over $L(x)$, containing $E$. The derivation $D =
d/dx$ of $K(x)$ extends uniquely to $E$ and to each $E_L$
\VolI{Theorem~3.2.3}, and
\[
  \Const_D(E_L) = L
\]
because a constant of $E_L$ is algebraic over $L$ \VolI{Lemma~3.3.2}.

We use the language of Chapter~\ref{ch:function-fields}. A \emph{place} always
means a place of $E_{\Kbar}$; it is \emph{finite} if it lies over a point $x
= x_0$ with $x_0 \in \Kbar$ and \emph{infinite} otherwise. Each place $P$ has a
ramification index $r_P$ over $\Kbar(x)$ and a uniformising parameter $\pi$,
and every $f \in E_{\Kbar}$ has an expansion as a Laurent series in $\pi$ with
coefficients in $\Kbar$, whose order is $\ord{P}(f)$. A \emph{differential} is
an expression $f\,dx$ with $f \in E_{\Kbar}$; at $P$ it can be written $(f\,
dx/d\pi)\, d\pi$, and its \emph{order} $\ord{P}(f\,dx)$ and its \emph{residue}
$\residue_P(f\,dx)$ are the order of the series $f\,dx/d\pi$ and its
coefficient of $\pi^{-1}$. Neither depends on the choice of $\pi$. Since $x -
x_0$ has order $r_P$ at a finite place and $x$ has order $-r_P$ at an infinite
one,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order-dx}
  \ord{P}(f\,dx) =
  \begin{cases}
    \ord{P}(f) + r_P - 1 & \text{if $P$ is finite,} \\
    \ord{P}(f) - r_P - 1 & \text{if $P$ is infinite.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For $u \in E_{\Kbar}$ we write $du$ for $(Du)\,dx$. We shall use four facts.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(D\arabic*)}]
\item\label{D:exact} If $\ord{P}(u) \ne 0$ then $\ord{P}(du) = \ord{P}(u) -
  1$, and if $\ord{P}(u) \ge 0$ then $\ord{P}(du) \ge 0$. In every case
  $\residue_P(du) = 0$.
\item\label{D:log} For $u \ne 0$, $\ord{P}(du/u) \ge -1$ and
  $\residue_P(du/u) = \ord{P}(u)$.
\item\label{D:const} An element of $E_{\Kbar}$ without poles is in $\Kbar$.
  Every $u \ne 0$ has a \emph{divisor} $(u) = \sum_P \ord{P}(u)\, P$, a finite
  sum; and $(u) = 0$ if and only if $u \in \Kbar$.
\item\label{D:residue} A differential has finitely many poles, and the sum of
  its residues over all places is zero.
\end{enumerate}
\ref{D:exact} and \ref{D:log} are read off the Laurent series, as in the
proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative}; \ref{D:const} and
\ref{D:residue} are theorems of Chapter~\ref{ch:function-fields}; all four
are Theorem~\ref{thm:differentials}.

A nonzero differential is \emph{of the first kind}\index{differential!of the first kind} if it has no poles, and
\emph{of the second kind} if all its residues are zero. The differentials of
the first kind form a vector space over $\Kbar$ whose dimension is the genus
of $E$. By~\ref{D:exact}, every nonzero $du$ is of the second kind; and $du$
has no poles only if $u$ has none, that is, only if $du = 0$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:running-differentials}
Let $E = \Q(x)[y]/(y^2 - (1 - x^3))$, the field of
Example~\ref{ex:running-hermite}. Over each $x_0$ with $x_0^3 \ne 1$ there are
two places, unramified, at which $y = \pm\sqrt{1 - x_0^3}$. Over each of the
three roots of $x^3 = 1$ there is one place, with $r_P = 2$ and $\ord{P}(y) =
1$. There is one infinite place $\infty$, with $r_\infty = 2$, $\ord{\infty}(x)
= -2$ and $\ord{\infty}(y) = -3$. From~\eqref{eq:order-dx} we find the
following.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
 & at $x_0^3 = 1$ & at $\infty$ & at $(x, y) = (0, \pm 1)$ \\
\midrule
order of $dx/y$      & $-1 + 1 = 0$ & $3 - 3 = 0$ & $0$ \\
order of $x\,dx/y$   & $0$          & $-2 + 3 - 3 = -2$ & $1$ \\
order of $dx/(x y)$  & $0$          & $2 + 3 - 3 = 2$ & $-1$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
So $dx/y$ is of the first kind. The differential $x\,dx/y$ has a single
pole, a double one at $\infty$; its residue there is zero by~\ref{D:residue},
so it is of the second kind. And $dx/(xy)$ has two simple poles, at $(0, 1)$
and $(0, -1)$, with residues $1$ and $-1$: at those places $x$ is a
uniformising parameter and $1/y = \pm 1$. These are the three behaviours the
algorithm has to tell apart.
\end{example}

\section{Liouville's theorem}\label{sec:liouville-theorem}

Elementary extensions, and what it means for $f$ to have an elementary
integral over a differential field, are defined in
\VolI{Definitions~5.1.3 and~5.1.4}. The theorem of Liouville, in the strong
form due to Risch, holds for any differential field of characteristic $0$
\VolI{Theorem~5.5.3}. For our fields it reads as follows.

\begin{theorem}[Liouville]\label{thm:liouville}\index{Liouville's theorem}\standing{cited}{\cite[Theorem~5.5.3]{bronstein2005}}
Let $f \in E$. If $f$ has an elementary integral over $E$, then there are $v
\in E$, constants $c_1, \dots, c_k \in \Kbar$, and $u_1, \dots, u_k \in
E_L^*$, where $L = K(c_1, \dots, c_k)$, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:liouville}
  f = Dv + \sum_{i=1}^k c_i\, \frac{Du_i}{u_i} .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Conversely, if~\eqref{eq:liouville} holds then $\int f\,dx = v + \sum_i c_i
\log u_i$ is elementary over $E$. So the integration problem for $E$ is the
problem of deciding whether~\eqref{eq:liouville} has a solution, and finding
one.

The representation~\eqref{eq:liouville} is far from unique, and a search
needs to know where to look for the $c_i$. The answer is that they are
dictated by the residues of $f\,dx$. Taking residues in~\eqref{eq:liouville}
at a place $P$ and using \ref{D:exact} and \ref{D:log},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residue-liouville}
  \residue_P(f\,dx) = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i\, \ord{P}(u_i) ,
\end{equation}
an integer combination of the $c_i$. The next theorem, which is Trager's
\cite[\S5.1]{trager1984}, says that one can do no better and need do no
worse.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:liouville-residues}\standing{proved}{}
Let $f \in E$ have an elementary integral over $E$. Let $V \subseteq \Kbar$
be the vector space over $\Q$ spanned by the residues of $f\,dx$, let $q_1,
\dots, q_k$ be a basis of $V$ over $\Q$, and let $K' = K(q_1, \dots, q_k)$,
which is the field generated over $K$ by the residues. For each place $P$
write $\residue_P(f\,dx) = \sum_j \rho_{Pj}\, q_j$ with $\rho_{Pj} \in \Q$.
Then there are $v \in E$, an integer $m > 0$ and $u_1, \dots, u_k \in
E_{K'}^*$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:liouville-basis}
  f = Dv + \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^k q_j\, \frac{Du_j}{u_j},
  \qquad (u_j) = m \sum_P \rho_{Pj}\, P \quad (1 \le j \le k).
\end{equation}
Here $v$ can be taken to be the $v$ of any representation~\eqref{eq:liouville}
with $v \in E$. Moreover, if~\eqref{eq:liouville} holds with $c_i \in L$ and
$u_i \in E_L$, then $K' \subseteq L$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The last statement is~\eqref{eq:residue-liouville}: every residue is in the
additive group generated by the $c_i$.

By Theorem~\ref{thm:liouville} we have~\eqref{eq:liouville} with $v \in E$,
$c_i \in L$ and $u_i \in E_L^*$ for some finite extension $L$ of $K$, and
then $K' \subseteq L$. The argument below leaves $v$ as it is. Extend $q_1, \dots, q_k$ by $b_1, \dots,
b_l$ to a basis over $\Q$ of the space spanned by $V$ and the $c_i$, and write
\[
  c_i = \sum_j r_{ij}\, q_j + \sum_h s_{ih}\, b_h, \qquad r_{ij}, s_{ih} \in \Q .
\]
Let $m > 0$ be an integer such that all $m r_{ij}$ and $m s_{ih}$ are
integers, and put $w_j = \prod_i u_i^{m r_{ij}}$ and $z_h = \prod_i u_i^{m
s_{ih}}$, which are in $E_L^*$. Since the logarithmic derivative turns
products into sums,
\[
  \sum_i c_i\, \frac{Du_i}{u_i}
  = \frac{1}{m} \Bigl( \sum_j q_j\, \frac{Dw_j}{w_j}
                     + \sum_h b_h\, \frac{Dz_h}{z_h} \Bigr) .
\]
Taking residues at a place $P$ as in~\eqref{eq:residue-liouville},
\[
  m\, \residue_P(f\,dx) = \sum_j q_j\, \ord{P}(w_j) + \sum_h b_h\, \ord{P}(z_h) .
\]
The left side is in $V$, and the $q_j$ and $b_h$ together are linearly
independent over $\Q$. So $\ord{P}(z_h) = 0$ for every $h$ and every $P$, and
$\ord{P}(w_j) = m \rho_{Pj}$. By~\ref{D:const} each $z_h$ is a constant, so
$Dz_h = 0$, and
\[
  f = Dv + \frac{1}{m} \sum_j q_j\, \frac{Dw_j}{w_j},
  \qquad (w_j) = m \sum_P \rho_{Pj}\, P .
\]
It remains to bring the $w_j$ down from $E_L$ to $E_{K'}$. Let $N$ and
$\operatorname{Tr}$ be the norm and trace from $E_L$ to $E_{K'}$, and $l = [L
: K'] = [E_L : E_{K'}]$. The trace commutes with $D$ \VolI{Theorem~3.2.4},
and $\operatorname{Tr}(Dw/w) = D(Nw)/Nw$, the norm being a product of
conjugates. Since $f$, $Dv$ and the $q_j$ are in $E_{K'}$, applying the trace
gives
\[
  l f = l\, Dv + \frac{1}{m} \sum_j q_j\, \frac{D(N w_j)}{N w_j} .
\]
This is~\eqref{eq:liouville-basis} with $lm$ in place of $m$ and $u_j = N
w_j$; the divisors of the $u_j$ are then forced, by taking residues once
more.
\end{proof}

Three things follow at once. The first is the minimal field of constants:
the integral can be written over $K'$, and over no field that fails to
contain $K'$. The second is the minimal number of logarithms, which is $k =
\dim_{\Q} V$: fewer would not span the residues. The third is that the divisors
of the $u_j$ are known in advance, up to the common factor $m$. What is not
known is whether those divisors, or some multiples of them, are divisors of
functions. That is the whole difficulty, and Chapters~\ref{ch:logarithmic-part}
and~\ref{ch:finite-order} are about it.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:second-kind}\standing{proved}{}
Let $f \in E$ be such that all residues of $f\,dx$ are zero. Then $f$ has an
elementary integral over $E$ if and only if $f = Dv$ for some $v \in E$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Here $V = 0$ and $k = 0$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:liouville-residues}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:first-kind}\standing{proved}{}
If $f \in E$ and $f\,dx$ is of the first kind, then $f$ has no elementary
integral over $E$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:second-kind} we would have $f\,dx = dv$ with $v \in E$.
By~\ref{D:exact}, $v$ has no poles, since $dv$ has none; so $v \in K$
by~\ref{D:const}, and $f = Dv = 0$, whereas a differential of the first kind
is not zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:first-kind}
By Example~\ref{ex:running-differentials}, $dx/\sqrt{1 - x^3}$ is of the first
kind. Hence
\[
  \int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 - x^3}}
\]
is not an elementary function, and neither is the integral
$\int dx/(1 - x^3)^{3/2}$ of Example~\ref{ex:running-hermite}, which
differs from a third of it by an algebraic function. The same argument
applies to $\int dx/\sqrt{p(x)}$ for any squarefree $p$ of degree at least
$3$: these are the integrals of the first kind on elliptic and hyperelliptic
curves.
\end{example}

No such shortcut settles the other two integrands of
Example~\ref{ex:running-differentials}. For $x\,dx/y$,
Corollary~\ref{cor:second-kind} leaves the question whether it is $dv$ for
some $v \in E$; for $dx/(xy)$, Theorem~\ref{thm:liouville-residues} leaves the
question whether some multiple of the divisor $(0, 1) - (0, -1)$ is the
divisor of a function.

\section{Outline of the algorithm}\label{sec:liouville-outline}

Theorem~\ref{thm:liouville-residues} shapes the algorithm. Given $f \in E$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Reduce the finite poles.} The Hermite reduction of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:hermite} writes $f = Dg + h$, where $h\,dx$ has at most
  simple poles at the finite places. This step never fails and decides
  nothing.
\item \emph{Find the algebraic part.} With the poles at infinity put out of
  the way by a change of variable, Chapter~\ref{ch:algebraic-part} shows
  that if $f$ has an elementary integral then the $v$
  of~\eqref{eq:liouville} is $g$, up to a constant, and $h\,dx$ has no poles
  at infinity. If $h\,dx$ does have a pole at infinity, the integral is not
  elementary. If $h = 0$, the integral is $g$.
\item \emph{Compute the residues.} The residues of $h\,dx$ are the roots of
  a polynomial computed by two resultants, without leaving $K$
  (Chapter~\ref{ch:logarithmic-part}). A basis $q_1, \dots, q_k$ of their span
  over $\Q$ gives the divisors $\delta_j = m \sum_P \rho_{Pj} P$
  of~\eqref{eq:liouville-basis}.
\item \emph{Find the logarithms.} For each $j$, decide whether some multiple
  $n_j \delta_j$ with $n_j > 0$ is the divisor of a function $u_j$, and find
  $u_j$ (Chapters~\ref{ch:divisors} and~\ref{ch:finite-order}). If for some
  $j$ there is none, the integral is not elementary.
\item \emph{Check.} If $h = \frac1m \sum_j (q_j / n_j)\, Du_j / u_j$, then
  \[
    \int f\,dx = g + \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^k \frac{q_j}{n_j} \log u_j .
  \]
  Otherwise the difference is a differential of the first kind, and the
  integral is not elementary.
\end{enumerate}
Steps~1 and~2 use an integral basis of $E$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:integral-bases}),
and step~2 needs it normal at infinity. Step~4 needs arithmetic on divisors
(Chapter~\ref{ch:divisors}) and a bound on the $n_j$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:finite-order}). When the curve has genus $0$ none of this
is needed: a rational parametrisation turns $f\,dx$ into a rational
differential (Chapter~\ref{ch:genus-zero}).

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}
Verify the table of Example~\ref{ex:running-differentials}, and add a row for
$dx/y^3$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Let $E = K(x)$. Show that Theorem~\ref{thm:liouville-residues} gives, for a
rational function $f$ with squarefree denominator and no polynomial part, the
statement that $\int f\,dx$ can be written with $\dim_{\Q} V$ logarithms, where
$V$ is spanned by the roots of the Rothstein--Trager resultant of
\VolI{\S2.4}, and with no fewer.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Let $y^2 = p(x)$ with $p \in K[x]$ squarefree of degree $2g + 1$ or $2g + 2$,
$g \ge 1$. Show that $x^i\,dx/y$ is of the first kind for $0 \le i < g$, and
is not for $i \ge g$.
\end{exercise}
