\chapter[Extensions of Monomial Extensions]{Algebraic Extensions of Monomial Extensions}\label{ch:monomial}

Part~II integrated functions on a curve over a field of constants. An
elementary function in general is built by several steps, each a logarithm,
an exponential or an algebraic function of what came before, and the
algebraic steps can come anywhere: $\sqrt{x + \log x}$ has its square root on
top of a logarithm. To integrate such a function one views it, as Risch did,
as an algebraic function of one variable over a field that is no longer a
field of constants: an element of a finite extension $E$ of $k(t)$, where
$t$ is a monomial over a differential field $k$ and $k$ contains everything
built before $t$.

This chapter sets up that view. It is the counterpart of
\VolI{Chapters~3 and~4}: places take the part of irreducible polynomials,
and the order function at a place takes the part of the order function at a
polynomial. Its results are the generalised residue and the residue
criterion, a necessary condition for an elementary integral that needs no
more than two resultants. The source is Part~1 and the beginning of Part~2
of Bronstein's paper~\cite{bronstein1990}; several of his statements are
proved here from Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative} in place of the Puiseux
expansions he uses.

\section{The setting}\label{sec:mono-setting}

Throughout Part~III, $(k, D)$ is a differential field of characteristic $0$,
$t$ is a monomial over $k$ \VolI{Definition~3.4.1}, and
\[
  E = k(t)[y]/(F)
\]
is a finite extension of $k(t)$ of degree $n$, with $F \in k[t][y]$ monic in
$y$ and irreducible over $\kbar(t)$, so that $k$ is algebraically closed in
$E$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:absolute-irreducibility}, whose proof does not use the
derivation). $D$ extends uniquely to $E$. The field of constants of $k$ is
$C$, and we assume $\Const_D(E) = C$. This is the setting of
Chapter~\ref{ch:hermite}, and everything proved there applies.

We are interested in three kinds of monomial, in the terminology of
\VolI{\S5.1}:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
 & $Dt$ & special irreducible polynomials \\
\midrule
$t = x$ & $1$ & none \\
$t$ a logarithm, $t = \log \eta$ & $D\eta/\eta$, with $\eta \in k^*$ & none \\
$t$ an exponential, $t = \exp \eta$ & $t\, D\eta$, with $\eta \in k$ & $t$ only \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The last column is \VolI{Theorems~5.1.1 and~5.1.2}. A \emph{place} of $E$ is a
place over $k$ (Section~\ref{sec:ff-places}); it is \emph{normal} if it is
finite and lies over a normal irreducible polynomial, and \emph{special} if
it lies over a special one. In the first two cases every finite place is
normal; in the exponential case the finite places are normal except those
over $t = 0$. The places at infinity behave like special places in all three
cases, and Bronstein calls them special too
\cite[\S1]{bronstein1990}.

What we know about derivatives at these places is the following.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:orders-all-places}\standing{cited}{Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative}; \cite[Lemmas~1.7 and~1.8]{bronstein1990}}
Let $P$ be a place of $E$ with ramification index $r$, and $f \in E$ with $Df
\ne 0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item If $P$ is normal: $\ord{P}(Df) = \ord{P}(f) - r$ if $\ord{P}(f) \ne 0$,
  and $\ord{P}(Df) \ge 1 - r$ otherwise.
\item If $t$ is an exponential and $P$ lies over $t = 0$ or is infinite:
  $\ord{P}(Df) = \ord{P}(f)$ if $\ord{P}(f) \ne 0$, and $\ord{P}(Df) \ge 0$
  otherwise.
\item If $t$ is a logarithm, or $t = x$, and $P$ is infinite: $\ord{P}(f) \le
  \ord{P}(Df) \le \ord{P}(f) + r$ if $\ord{P}(f) \ne 0$, and $\ord{P}(Df) \ge
  0$ otherwise.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Part~(i) is Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative}. Parts~(ii) and~(iii) are
Bronstein's Lemma~1.8, proved by expanding $f$ in powers of $t^{\pm 1/r}$ and
differentiating term by term; in~(ii) the equality holds because a
cancellation of the leading term would make the product of a power of $t$
and an element of $\kbar$ a constant, against the assumption that $t$ is a monomial with
$\Const_D(k(t)) = C$. For $t = x$, (iii) is the statement that differentiation
raises the order at infinity by exactly $r$ when the order is not zero
(Lemma~4.3 of~\cite{trager1984}); for a logarithm the order can rise by less,
because the coefficients of the expansion are no longer constants.

\section{Simple elements and the Hermite reduction}\label{sec:mono-hermite}

\begin{definition}\label{def:simple}
An element $f \in E$ is \emph{simple} if $\ord{P}(f) \ge -r_P$ at every
normal place $P$.
\end{definition}

For $E = k(t)$ these are the simple functions of Volume~I
\VolI{Corollary~4.4.1}. Bronstein's word for it is \emph{normal}; we keep
``normal'' for polynomials and places.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:simple}\standing{proved}{}
Let $w$ be an integral basis of $E$ over $k(t)$, and let $d$ be the
denominator of $f \in E$ with respect to $w$, with splitting factorisation $d
= d_s d_n$. Then $f$ is simple if and only if $d_n$ is squarefree. In
particular, the $h$ returned by \seealg{HermiteReduce}, and by
\seealg{LazyHermiteReduce} when the basis it returns is integral, is simple.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At a normal place $P$ over the irreducible $p$, the order of a polynomial is
$r_P$ times its multiplicity at $p$, and $d_s$ has order $0$. If $d_n$ is
squarefree, then $\ord{P}(d) \le r_P$, and $d f \in \OO$, so $\ord{P}(f) \ge
-r_P$. Conversely, let $f$ be simple and let $d_n^*$ be the squarefree part of
$d_n$. Then $d_n^* d_s f$ has nonnegative order at every normal place,
because $f$ is simple, and at every special finite place, because $d f \in
\OO$ and $d_n$ has order $0$ there. So $d_n^* d_s f \in \OO = \OO_w$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:places}(ii), and $d$ divides $d_n^* d_s$.
\end{proof}

This is Proposition~1.18 of~\cite{bronstein1990}. So the first step of the
integration of an element of $E$ is the Hermite reduction of
Chapter~\ref{ch:hermite}, exactly as in Part~II: it produces $g$ and a simple
$h$ with $f = Dg + h$, and it decides nothing.

\section{The generalised residue}\label{sec:mono-residue}

On a curve over constants, the residue of $f\,dx$ at a place over $x_0$ where
$f$ has at most a simple pole is the value of $r\,(x - x_0)\,f$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:residue-value}). Over a monomial extension there is no
differential to take residues of, but the same expression makes sense.

\begin{definition}\label{def:tau}
Let $P$ be a normal place of $E$ over the monic irreducible $p \in k[t]$,
with ramification index $r$, and let $f \in E$ with $\ord{P}(f) \ge -r$. The
\emph{residue} of $f$ at $P$ is
\[
  \tau_P(f) = \Bigl( r\, f\, \frac{p}{Dp} \Bigr)(P) \in \kbar ,
\]
the value at $P$ of a function that has no pole there, because $Dp$ does not
vanish at $P$.
\end{definition}

This is Definition~2.3 of~\cite{bronstein1990}, and for $E = k(t)$ it is the
residue of \VolI{Definition~4.4.1}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:tau}\standing{proved}{}
Let $P$ be a normal place with ramification index $r$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $\tau_P$ is additive, and $\tau_P(c f) = c\, \tau_P(f)$ for a constant
  $c$.
\item For $u \in E^*$, $\ord{P}(Du/u) \ge -r$ and $\tau_P(Du/u) = \ord{P}(u)$.
\item If $v \in E$ has no pole at $P$, then $\ord{P}(Dv) \ge 1 - r$ and
  $\tau_P(Dv) = 0$.
\item If $f = g/d$ with $d \in k[t]$, $g \in E$ without pole at $P$, and $p$
  divides $d$ exactly once, then $\tau_P(f) = r\,(g/Dd)(P)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) is clear. Take $\sigma$, $\alpha$ and $\pi$ as in
Theorem~\ref{thm:puiseux}, so that $\pi^r = t - \alpha$, and let $\Delta$ be
the derivation of $\kbar((\pi))$ constructed in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative}. There $\Delta\pi / \pi = D(t - \alpha) / (r
\pi^r)$, and $D(t - \alpha)$ has a nonzero value $\epsilon$ at $P$ because
$p$ is normal. Writing $p = (t - \alpha)\, p_1$, we get $Dp \equiv D(t -
\alpha)\, p_1$ modulo $\pi^r$, so $p/Dp$ is $\pi^r/\epsilon$ times a series
with constant term $1$.

(ii) Let $u = \pi^\nu u_0$ with $u_0$ of order $0$. Then $Du/u = \nu\,
\Delta\pi/\pi + \Delta u_0 / u_0$. The first term is $\nu \epsilon / (r
\pi^r)$ times a series with constant term $1$, and the second has order at
least $1 - r$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative}(ii). Multiplying by $r\,
p/Dp$ gives $\nu$ plus terms of positive order.

(iii) is Lemma~\ref{lem:order-derivative}(i) and~(ii), and then $r\,(Dv)\,
p/Dp$ has positive order.

(iv) Write $d = p\, c$ with $c$ prime to $p$. Then $Dd \equiv (Dp)\, c$ modulo
$p$, so $p/Dp$ and $d/Dd$ differ by a factor with value $1$ at $P$, and $r f
p/Dp$ has the value of $r g/Dd$.
\end{proof}

These are Lemma~2.7 and Propositions~2.4, 2.5 and~2.8 of~\cite{bronstein1990}.
Liouville's theorem holds over $E$ as over any differential field
\VolI{Theorem~5.5.3}: if $f \in E$ has an elementary integral over $E$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:liouville-mixed}
  f = Dv + \sum_{i=1}^m c_i\, \frac{Du_i}{u_i}
\end{equation}
with $v \in E$, constants $c_i$ algebraic over $C$, and $u_i \in E(c_1, \dots,
c_m)^*$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:tau-liouville}\standing{proved}{}
Let $f \in E$ be simple and suppose that~\eqref{eq:liouville-mixed} holds.
Then $v$ has no pole at any normal place, and at every normal place $P$
\[
  \tau_P(f) = \sum_{i=1}^m c_i\, \ord{P}(u_i) .
\]
In particular $\tau_P(f)$ is a constant.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If $\ord{P}(v) < 0$ at a normal place, then $\ord{P}(Dv) = \ord{P}(v) - r <
-r$, while $f$ and the $Du_i/u_i$ have order at least $-r$; so $v$ has no
pole. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:tau}.
\end{proof}

\section{The residue criterion}\label{sec:mono-criterion}

The residues are computed as in Chapter~\ref{ch:logarithmic-part}. Let $f$
be simple, with numerator $\sum_i a_i w_i$ and denominator $d = d_s d_n$ with
respect to an integral basis $w$, and write $\sum_i a_i w_i / d_s = G/H$ with
$G \in k[t][y]$ and $H \in k[t]$. Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-resultant}
  R(Z) = \resultant_t\bigl( \operatorname{pp}_Z\bigl(
           \resultant_y( G - Z\, H\, Dd_n,\; F ) \bigr),\; d_n \bigr) \in k[Z] .
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[the residue criterion]\label{thm:residue-criterion}\standing{proved}{}
With the notation above, the roots of $R$ in $\kbar$ are the numbers
$\tau_P(f)/r_P$, for $P$ over the roots of $d_n$. If $f$ has an elementary
integral over $E$, then all the roots of $R$ are constants; equivalently, $R
= a\, R_0$ with $a \in k$ and $R_0 \in \overline{C}[Z]$ monic.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The first statement is the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:residue-polynomial}
with $k$ in place of $K$, $Dd_n$ in place of $d'$, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:tau}(iv) in place of Lemma~\ref{lem:residue-value}: over
$\kbar$, the function $a = d_n f / Dd_n$ has no pole at the places over the
roots of $d_n$, its values there are the $\tau_P(f)/r_P$, and its
characteristic polynomial specialises at each root to the product of $Z -
a(P)$ with multiplicities $r_P$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:puiseux-embeddings}). The
second is Corollary~\ref{cor:tau-liouville}.
\end{proof}

This is Propositions~2.6 and~2.9 of~\cite{bronstein1990}, which generalise
the criterion of Rothstein for transcendental integrands
\VolI{Theorem~5.6.1}. The condition is tested without factoring $R$: with
$\kappa$ the derivation of $k[Z]$ that differentiates the coefficients, all
the roots of $R$ are constants if and only if $R$ divides $\kappa(R)$ in
$k[Z]$~\cite[\S3.8]{bronstein1998tutorial}. The criterion is necessary and
not sufficient. Its value is that it often ends the computation at once.

\begin{example}\label{ex:criterion-fails}
This is the first example of~\cite[\S2]{bronstein1990}. Consider
\[
  \int \frac{\log x + \sqrt{\log x + \sqrt{\log x}}}{1 + \log x}\, dx .
\]
Let $k = \Q(x)$, $t = \log x$, and $E = k(t)[y]/(F)$ with $F = y^4 - 2 t y^2 +
t^2 - t$, of which $\sqrt{t + \sqrt t}$ is a root. The integrand is $f = (t +
y)/(1 + t)$. With respect to $(1, y, y^2, y^3)$ its denominator is $d = 1 +
t$, which is normal, and $F(-1, y) = y^4 + 2y^2 + 2$ is squarefree, so the
places over $t = -1$ are unramified and $f$ is simple. Here $Dd = 1/x$, $G =
t + y$ and $H = 1$, and
\begin{align*}
  R(Z) &= \resultant_t\Bigl( \resultant_y\bigl( t + y - \tfrac{Z}{x},\; F \bigr),\; 1 + t \Bigr) \\
       &= \frac{Z^4 + 4x Z^3 + 8x^2 Z^2 + 8x^3 Z + 5x^4}{x^4} .
\end{align*}
Its roots are $x$ times the roots of $W^4 + 4W^3 + 8W^2 + 8W + 5$, and are not
constants. Accordingly $R$ does not divide $\kappa(R)$: the remainder is
$4\,(Z^3 + 4x Z^2 + 6x^2 Z + 5x^3)/x^4$. So the integral is not elementary, and
this was decided by two resultants: no integral basis, no divisor, no
question of finite order.
\end{example}

\section{What remains}\label{sec:mono-remains}

When the criterion is passed, the residues $\tau_P(f)$ at the normal places
are constants, and Corollary~\ref{cor:tau-liouville} gives the orders of the
$u_i$ at the normal places as in Chapter~\ref{ch:logarithmic-part}. Three
things are new, compared with Part~II, and they are the subject of the next
two chapters.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The places that are not normal, those at infinity and, for an
  exponential, those over $t = 0$, cannot be moved out of the way. In
  Part~II a change of variable made infinity an ordinary point; here $1/(t -
  N)$ is not a monomial of the same kind as $t$. The orders of the $u_i$ at
  those places have to be found by other means.
\item Some of the $u_i$ may lie in $k$, or in $\kbar$, and have no zeros or
  poles on the curve at all. They contribute nothing to any residue, and
  are found by a recursive call in $k$.
\item The algebraic part $v$ is no longer $g$ plus a constant. Its
  coefficients in an integral basis satisfy differential equations over $k$,
  or over $k(t)$.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}
Show that for $E = k(t)$ Definition~\ref{def:tau} is the residue of
\VolI{Definition~4.4.1}, and Theorem~\ref{thm:residue-criterion} is the residue
criterion of \VolI{Theorem~5.6.1}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Let $k = \Q(x)$, $t = \log x$ and $y^2 = t$. Compute $\tau_P$ of $f = y/(x\,(t
- 1))$ at the two places over $t = 1$, and the polynomial $R$. Is the
criterion passed?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Apply the residue criterion to $\int dx / ((x - 1)\sqrt{\log x})$.
\end{exercise}
